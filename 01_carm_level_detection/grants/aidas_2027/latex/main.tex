{\small
{\bfseries Abstract.} Wrong-level spine surgery is a never event, unpaid by Medicare since 2009 [21] and reported to the
Joint Commission 9 times in 2024, 7 of them spinal [22]; paid US malpractice claims for surgical never events reached
\$1.3 billion over 1990--2010 [23]. It persists because surgeons confirm the level by counting vertebrae on an X-ray
from a C-arm, the operating room's mobile fluoroscope, and the count fails in patients with an extra or missing lumbar
segment and on images that show too few vertebrae. This project's goal is a customizable, locally hosted
retrieval-augmented language model for spine care (a RAG-LLM): a multimodal model on the WSU grid that reads the image through the Open Spine Consortium's X-ray detector and level model, retrieves from the consortium's index of Scopus
and ScienceDirect spine literature (text, images and multimedia, under WSU's text and data mining rights) and answers
with verbatim, cited quotations; fine-tuned, in its second release, on images paired with clinical charts under the consortium's pending IRB protocol (about 4,000 Detroit Medical Center spine surgery patients; amended as the work requires); compared with OpenEvidence's clinical evidence service through a Claude plug-in [31]; and customizable in that a lab swaps in its
own corpus, detectors and records. The consortium's spine program is the case example of what an organized,
AI-assisted workflow builds in a short time: an imaging platform (211 catalogued sources, 115 on the grid at 5.3 TB: a census of 21,622 public CTs mined for the anatomy on which counting fails, 39,705 plain radiographs,
COBRA's 692 radiotherapy cone-beam cases with 375,618 projections); 802 CTs with every vertebra labeled
(CTSpinoPelvic1K, a NeurIPS 2026 benchmark); a physics renderer; and a level model that, never trained on a C-arm
image, names the level of 96.4\pct{} of the lateral vertebrae a film-trained detector finds on a public C-arm dataset,
while detection on that C-arm stays at 80.5\pct{} (90.6\pct{} at a lower threshold) against the 97\pct{} set in
advance, 145 of COBRA's 692 cases among the frames that carry it that far. Aim 1 studies the workflow: an AI
agent iterates the scientific method inside the consortium's frameworks (the cluster's job rules, a code gate,
reporting protocols, multi-agent coordination on GitHub), a result counting only when a test sealed before the run
passes and a person merges it, on tasks with a planted fault submitting a wrong result, we expect, in under a fifth of
runs against over half without the checks; from the same records Aim 1 measures what each run costs in tokens, tool
calls and context, and Symphony's claim protocol against the worktree and framework orchestrators it competes with, on CooperBench's pairs and SWE-bench. Aim 2 gives the agent to eight medical trainees and measures, task
against matched task, what it adds over a coding assistant; their literature questions go to the RAG-LLM on the grid. Aim 3 builds the case example through to the RAG-LLM: the detection gap closed with 372,000 unseen COBRA projections, learned X-ray simulation and a CT-paired cadaver set imaged at WSU, then the multimodal
model that reads the image and the chart. Deliverables: Aim 1's tasks, records, benchmark suites and report (MIT
license, as WSU's technology-transfer review clears), the RAG-LLM's first release on the public corpus with its graded
question set, the cadaver set, an FDA Pre-Submission, and NSF CAREER and NIH R21 proposals.

\section*{Project narrative}

\subsection*{1. Significance and innovation}
Surgeons confirm the level of a spine operation by counting vertebrae on an X-ray from a C-arm, the operating room's
mobile fluoroscope. The count fails often enough to matter: a 27-hospital Japanese registry recorded wrong-level
surgery in 0.12\pct{} of 34,544 patients having degenerative spine surgery [1]; US
neurosurgeons reported 418 wrong-level operations in about 1.3 million, one in 3,110, 17\pct{} followed by legal action or a settlement, and about half of respondents report at least one [2,3]. It fails most on a transitional vertebra, part
lumbar and part sacral: 12.9\pct{} of 550 surgical patients had one, and their levels were miscounted in clinic in
54\pct{} of them [4]. On 62 hard intraoperative images, surgeons' median naming error was 30.4\pct{}
unaided and 2.4\pct{} with LevelCheck [6], which registers the preoperative CT to each image [5] in 17 to 72\,s, at
the edge of the 20\,s to one minute surgeons said they would wait [7]. The hospital's stake is
written in rules: wrong-site surgery is a serious reportable event, unpaid by Medicare since January 2009 [21]; the
Joint Commission's 2024 review of voluntarily reported sentinel events holds 127 wrong surgeries, 9
wrong-level, 7 of those spinal, 36\pct{} with severe permanent harm [22]; paid US malpractice claims for surgical never
events totaled \$1.3 billion over 9,744 claims in 1990--2010 [23], 978 English spinal negligence claims over five
years \pounds535.5 million [24], and one US insurer's 68 wrong-site claims closed in 2013--2020 averaged \$136,453, spine the
commonest procedure [25].

The Open Spine Consortium at Wayne State has built the parts of a check that needs no CT, only the C-arm image, and
the data platform under it: a card for each of 211 imaging sources, 115 of them on the grid (5.3 TB), every item keyed to a person, nine leakage checks (seven run on the frozen split), and 563 quoted numbers audited against their models' training persons. Its CT census, 21,622 CTs from 21,603 persons, was mined for the anatomy on which counting fails: two automatic labelers chosen to fail differently filled a registry of 6,108 persons from the collections then in hand, 146 of them with a sixth lumbar vertebra (Figure~1d), 103 on an expert read, and 14 with a thirteenth thoracic, and screened 18 further public collections (843 GPU-hours) under a stopping rule, fixed before any download, that turned 29,479 mostly chest CTs away. A labeler's 9.3\pct{} rate of thirteenth rib-bearing vertebrae in 367 chest CTs fell to 1.4\pct{} (5 of 367) once a rib on the named body was required, the check outside the computation that this project's gates make routine. From the platform come CTSpinoPelvic1K, 802 CTs with every vertebra and the sacrum labeled [8],
whose benchmark paper is a NeurIPS 2026 poster; 39,705 plain radiographs, 12,709 of which train a film detector that
finds 98.8\pct{} (at 0.25) of 161 lumbar vertebrae on 17 spines it never saw; the physics renders and real frames of Aim 3,
COBRA's 692 cases among them [29]; and a level model that names 96.4\pct{} of the lateral vertebrae the
film detector finds on a public C-arm it never saw, while detection on a C-arm held out of training stays
at 80.5\pct{} (90.6\pct{} at a lower threshold), short of the 97\pct{} set in advance (Figure~1f). Closing that gap takes many GPU jobs run, checked and recorded on a shared cluster; the
consortium's 2026 trainee program hit the same wall, relying on mentors expert in Python, imaging and cluster
computing [9].

Our premise is that the bottleneck has moved from the model to the experiments; that an AI agent can run them if
strict frameworks, not the model, decide which results count; that the same frameworks, which record every run,
let us measure the agents; and that what such a workflow builds in a year is best shown on one case, this spine program,
whose end product is a customizable, locally hosted RAG-LLM: the consortium's detectors reading the image, its indexed
literature answering with cited quotations, a multimodal model fine-tuned on images paired with charts (a stated purpose of the pending protocol), compared with OpenEvidence's evidence service through a Claude plug-in [31], with no
indexed passage and no patient record leaving the grid. Three aims test it (Figure~1). \aim{Aim 1} has the agent iterate the scientific method
inside the consortium's frameworks: Beethoven runs Claude Code on the grid and keeps the grid's profile as checked data
and its job rules in writing; beethoven-qa checks the agent's code; beethoven-research holds REFORMS [10] rules for
machine-learning science and plans the CLAIM [11] checklist for imaging AI; and Symphony (public, MIT) gives each
piece of work to one agent through a claim that is a git branch, which GitHub lets exactly one of two racing agents
create, with the board and the release of idle claims as deterministic CI jobs and no server. The methodological contribution measures \emph{framework-bound scientific iteration}: how many wrong but
plausible results a research agent submits when the rules are only written, and when checks enforce them, and, from
the same records, what its work costs and how the protocol fares against the orchestrators it competes with.
Benchmarks score agents' code edits [12], machine-learning engineering [13] or papers [14], and studies of research
agents probe their leakage and tampering [15,16]; CooperBench found two agents in separate workspaces,
talking only by message, passing 183 of 652 feature pairs against 315 for one (GPT-5), most failures duplicated or
incompatible work [27]. We plant the faults an agent inherits from a lab's data and code, count those it passes on,
and meter the coordination. \aim{Aim 2} puts the agent in medical trainees' hands through Beethoven (Figure~1b).
\aim{Aim 3} builds the case example: it turns the agent on the detection gap, with COBRA's unused projections, a paired
CT and C-arm cadaver set imaged at WSU and a differentiable 3-D Gaussian X-ray renderer, as in [17,18], built on the
PI's Gaussian-splatting work, and then on the multimodal model that reads the image and the chart.

\subsection*{2. Aims, approach and preliminary work}
\aim{Aim 1 (Foundational AI, AI for Science): semi-autonomous research under strict frameworks, and the measurement of
the agents.} The agent is Claude Code at a pinned model version. Semi-autonomous: a person sets each task's question
and gate (a pass/fail test, sealed by hash before the first job, that the agent can read but not change) and merges
each result; any other step a person takes, blind to faults, is a logged intervention. The agent iterates (Figure~1a): it seals
each hypothesis with its criterion, runs jobs under Beethoven's rules, counts a result only from code beethoven-qa
passed (contracts searched for counterexamples by CrossHair and Z3) and keeps its REFORMS record, until the gate passes
or the budget is spent, then marks its Symphony pull request ready. \emph{1a, framework-bound iteration.} Thirty tasks,
10 for development, come from questions Aim 3 and the consortium have answered. Each of the 20 sealed has a hidden
check, a known answer the agent cannot read, and a clean and a faulted copy: once the checks freeze, someone who did
not write them plants a fault, a patient in both training and test sets, the wrong label scheme, a threshold tuned on
the test set, a typed number, or a run that does not reproduce. Both arms keep the model, Beethoven, the written rules
and eight GPU-hours a task, reruns included; one adds the checks (sealed hypotheses, beethoven-qa, split and rerun
checks); each runs each copy once (80 runs). \emph{1b, the cost of the agent.} Symphony records
what each run cost (a usage trailer on every commit, a usage artifact per Actions run); 1b adds a profiler (agentprof's hooks [28], system-call tracing) that logs every tool call, file read and
token to a reproducible trace, and has the agent run, as sealed tasks whose gate is the same tests passing at lower
cost, four economies: context compression, fewer tool calls, less code reading (a content-hashed index of the
repository and retrieval over it) and profiled prompts; and a standing routine proposes the next experiment from the last record: continuous, autonomous research under the gates. \emph{1c, the protocol
against its competitors.} The alternative to a claim protocol is an orchestrator: a script that fans tasks out to
agents in git worktrees, merges, reviews adversarially and verifies, as the consortium's own first attempt did; frameworks that make such a script durable (LangGraph's checkpointed fan-out, Temporal's sagas, OpenHands sandboxes, typed outputs); or a shared workspace with optimistic concurrency, which raised a worktree baseline from 63.8 to 82.5\pct{}
on Commit0-Lite [26]. 1c runs Symphony, the worktree orchestrator and one framework orchestrator on the same suites in
the same Actions environment, coordbench on CooperBench's pairs first [27], then SWE-bench instances [12] and Aim 3's
tasks, scoring pass rate, tokens, dollars, wall time, conflicts and duplicated work, and places each system, with
gh-aw's work queue, unsorry, Copilot's cloud agent and Claude Code's agent teams, on the twelve-axis taxonomy written for the comparison. Compute is GitHub's free standard runners
on public repositories and the grid; inference is line C1's seats, no API key. \emph{1d, the
control plane.} A web control panel driven by Claude's own browser (repositories, board, CI, tasks, workers, prompts)
showing each run's cost beside the same work on unsorry or Copilot's cloud agent; a wizard storing a lab's Claude
tokens as saved environment secrets; Beethoven bridging Actions to the grid, a worker in Actions submitting the Slurm
job and posting its results and logs back to the pull request; and the extension's one click brought from Claude Code
to the claude.ai Project each trainee works in. \emph{Preliminary work.} Built: Beethoven's grid profile and Claude Code worker (a Slurm job),
beethoven-qa's gate on Beethoven's agent and research code, the patient-split check, Symphony (its tests catch all 93
mutated rules; every interleaving of a few workers is checked against an oracle from its spec), coordbench and the taxonomy. The consortium builds the other
REFORMS checks, the CLAIM checklist, one-command reruns of gated code, the profiler hooks, the control panel, the
wizard and the bridge in kind. \emph{H1:} the checks
cut wrong results on faulted copies from over half to under 20\pct{}, with no fewer passes on clean copies, and each
accepted number reruns within a sealed tolerance. \emph{H1b:} the four economies together pass the same gates at under
half the tokens. \emph{H1c:} the protocol's cost per passing task is at or under each orchestrator's on every suite,
with pass rates inside their paired intervals; a loss is reported as one. \emph{Measures:} wrong results submitted,
clean copies passed, faults caught by kind and check, reruns, interventions, GPU-hours per accepted result; for 1b and
1c, tokens, tool calls, context and dollars per passing gate, pass rate, conflicts and wall time, paired by task and
suite.

\aim{Aim 2 (Education and Workforce): medical trainees drive the agent.} \emph{Approach.} Eight medical students and
residents in two waves of four \tbd{confirm} each receive a grid account, Beethoven and a gated sub-project; the agent runs the experiments inside the frameworks, the trainee owns the question and the
result, and a mentor reviews it. Literature questions go to the RAG-LLM's text half, a language model on the grid that quotes verbatim, with
citation and DOI, from the consortium's index of spine literature (Scopus, and ScienceDirect's text, images and
multimedia) under WSU's text and data mining and AI rights from Elsevier; no passage trains a model or
reaches an outside AI service; a misquoted passage fails a gate, and the mentor marks any quotation that does not support the answer. In week 1 each trainee does two
matched starter tasks, none of Aim 1's: one with the agent, one with the 2026 setup (Claude as a coding assistant, the
trainee submitting the jobs), task and order counterbalanced; the comfort survey runs before and after each wave; an
IRB determination is requested in month 1. \emph{Preliminary work} [9]: in 2026, 16 students, seven with no
coding and eleven with no imaging experience, worked on ten sub-projects in that setup; all six domains improved on
retrospective ratings (Figure~1c), all ten were submitted to a national meeting as abstracts, and the report's own limits, no
control group and no objective measure, are what Aim 2 adds. \emph{H2:}
with the agent, trainees pass more starter gates without a mentor's edit and reach each task's first passing gate
sooner; with eight trainees an estimate with 95\pct{} intervals that sizes the CyberTraining proposal.
\emph{Measures:} H2's two, misquoted or unsupported quotations and comfort (secondary), in a preregistered
within-trainee crossover.

\aim{Aim 3 (AI for Health): the case example, from labeled 3-D structure to 2-D fluoroscopy to a spine RAG-LLM.} \emph{Data.} Physics renders
of CTSpinoPelvic1K, VerSe and TotalSegmentator CTs (7,045 standard and 1,482 transitional annotated frames) and a
device-styled corpus of 142 TotalSegmentator spines; real frames of three devices: C-ArmLSpine (149 public lumbar
cases, each a cone-beam CT with AP and lateral frames; truth automatic, two CT labelers agreeing) [30], DeepFluoro (cadaver pelvis fluoroscopy) and COBRA (radiotherapy imaging) [29]. \emph{Model}: a
detector finds each body and the sacrum, a level model names each body, a count up from the sacrum checks the names,
and the stack abstains where count and shape disagree or the name is unsure. New here, the C-arm detector will learn
each body's four corners, as the film detector does, from the CT's corners projected at the device's geometry,
exact on renders. \emph{Preliminary results} (Figure~1f). With no C-arm image in training, the level
model names 96.4\pct{} of the 617 lateral bodies the film-trained detector finds on C-ArmLSpine (88.0\pct{} of all 676
with agreed truth) but 43.7\pct{} of the 631 AP bodies. Fine-tuned on the AP frames of five cases, it names
90.8\pct{}, and 98.1\pct{} with all other cases, each test case held out. On the 233 frames where the sacrum is found
(81.5\pct{} of the 286 that show one), cross-validated models, checked against the count, name 98.2\pct{} of 1,009
bodies, and the stack abstains on 9.9\pct{} of those frames, catching five of the six it would have named wrongly.
Detection does not carry: trained on two other devices' real frames, the C-arm detector finds 80.5\pct{} of
C-ArmLSpine's vertebrae and 76.8\pct{} of its sacra at 0.74, a cross-device threshold chosen on other devices
(90.6\pct{} and 83.9\pct{} at 0.25). \emph{What COBRA adds.} COBRA is 692 radiotherapy
cone-beam cases (467 Elekta XVI, 225 Varian OBI), 375,618 projections, each case with its acquisition geometry and a registered planning CT [29]; 145 Elekta cases, 24 frames each, are in any frame bank (110 of them, 2,640 frames, train the detector), and 322 Elekta and all 225 Varian cases train nothing. Those frames carry detection today: Elekta frames and their styled renders alone find
86.8\pct{} (249 of 287 [81.1, 91.8]) of the held-out C-arm's vertebrae at 0.25 on outline truth, cadaver C-arm frames
alone 12.9\pct{}, and cutting the Elekta training frames from 2,640 to 240 costs 47.7 points [$-$53.7, $-$41.8]. The 372,000
unseen projections, with a CT whose automatic labels project onto every one, are real-image corner labels for the new detector
at scale, paired data to fit the simulator's spectrum, scatter and sensor terms, and a second held-out
vendor (Varian); the cost is export, storage, a quality look at the Varian bank and GPU hours, an Aim 1 task set. Its limits: a linac's kV imager is not a surgical C-arm; a
detector that never saw it finds 6.2\pct{} of its vertebrae (18.5\pct{} at 0.25), the median age of its exported cases is 66 (known for 115 of 145), 7 transitional persons are found and 91 calls unread, and naming on it is weak today (the sacrum defined on 18.2\pct{} of
609 test frames): COBRA trains detection and simulation while the surgical C-arm and the cadaver set test naming. \emph{Closing the
gap.} Today's renders hurt: added to real frames, styled renders lower detection from 80.5 to 63.1\pct{} at 0.74
(90.6 to 87.8\pct{} at 0.25), and a classifier tells them from real frames 99 to 100\pct{} of the time. Physics
simulation (DeepDRR [19]) with strong domain randomization has matched real-data training for hip landmarks in
cadaver X-rays (SyntheX [20]); our device cards fitted to four imagers already reproduce held-out real frames at $R^2$
0.679 to 0.974. We randomize devices' geometry and spectra likewise, hold out each device in turn, and train 3-D
Gaussians to fit each sample in a subset of the labeled CTs, learning only spectrum, scatter and sensor
response until that classifier is at chance, so the anatomy and its labels stay exact. \emph{Cadaver set.} Two
specimens from WSU's Body Bequest Program are CT-scanned and imaged AP and lateral at lumbar and thoracic positions
on WSU's C-arm. A frame grabber records the video output, the set is sealed by hash before any model sees it, and
registering each specimen's CT to its frames [5] projects the CT's labels onto every frame. The intended use is an
advisory level label on each C-arm image, which the surgeon confirms; two deployment routes have FDA precedents, an
app on the C-arm maker's own platform and an external unit reading the C-arm's video output. \emph{The RAG-LLM} (Figure~1e). The detector and level model become tools of a multimodal model hosted on the grid that
takes the image, the detectors' boxes and names and the retrieved passages, and answers a clinical question with the
level, the finding and verbatim quotations. Its first release is fine-tuned on the public corpus (CTSpinoPelvic1K, the
labeled films, COBRA), its second on images paired with operative notes and radiology reports under
IRB-26-03-8648, whose stated purposes include a locally hosted multimodal spine LLM, amended as the work requires; a lab swaps
in its own corpus, detectors and records. On a held-out question set the mentors write on public images, its answers and OpenEvidence's, through a Claude plug-in [31], are graded side by side for correctness and for quotations that support them; the detectors' reading of an image may go to OpenEvidence with the question, while no indexed passage and no patient record leaves the grid. \emph{H3:} on each held-out
C-arm the detector finds 97\pct{} of vertebrae and of sacra with no more false detections than today's at 0.74; the stack names a frame in under 1\,s on one GPU (no latency of the stack is on file yet); on every cadaver frame it names the right level or abstains;
and on the question set the RAG-LLM is graded correct at least as often as OpenEvidence where the question needs the
image, with no unsupported quotation. \emph{Measures:} recall at 0.74 and 0.25, false detections, exact level,
abstention, corner error, latency (median, 95th percentile), render-to-real separability, graded correctness and
quotation support against OpenEvidence, leave-one-device-out with case-clustered intervals, the cadaver set opened
once, models hashed first.

\subsection*{3. Expected outcomes, deliverables and timeline}
H1 to H3 and the measures above are written before any run; none is a powered test; H1 and H2 size the CAREER and
CyberTraining proposals. Within the award: Aim 1's tasks, records and report, its benchmark suites (efficiency,
orchestration, the taxonomy) released through coordbench, the control panel, the wizard and the grid bridge; the
cohort's outcomes; the RAG-LLM's first release, on the public corpus, with its question set and grades; the cadaver
set, deposited beside CTSpinoPelvic1K as the Body Bequest Program's terms allow, and a transfer and latency
manuscript; an FDA Pre-Submission; and the CAREER and R21. The seed buys the researcher's hours,
subscriptions, the cadaver set and regulatory review; the trainee program, grid, Beethoven, Symphony, pipelines, the RAG-LLM's first release and faculty time come in kind; the Gaussian renderer is fitted as a feasibility test, its full build left to the R21. \emph{Quarter 1:} missing checks built, tasks
and gates sealed, faults planted, the profiler and the COBRA export, recruitment, bookings. \emph{Quarter 2:} Aim 1's runs (months 4--8), 1b's routine, the wizard and the grid bridge, wave 1 (months 4--7), cadaver imaging, transfer and latency results,
Pre-Submission. \emph{Quarter 3:} CAREER, 1c's suites, wave 2 (months 8--11), the renderer's fit, the control panel, the RAG-LLM's
