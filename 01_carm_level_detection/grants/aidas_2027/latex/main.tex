\vspace{6pt}
{\bfseries Abstract.} Wrong-level spine surgery persists because surgeons confirm the level by counting vertebrae on
an intraoperative X-ray from a C-arm, the operating room's mobile fluoroscope, and the count fails in patients with
an extra or missing lumbar segment and on images that show too few vertebrae. The Open Spine Consortium at Wayne
State has built the parts of an automated check: 802 CTs with every vertebra labeled, a renderer that turns them into
simulated C-arm images, and a model that names the level of 96.4\pct{} of the lateral vertebrae it detects on a
public C-arm dataset (88.0\pct{} of all of them) without having seen a C-arm image in training. One gap stands
between that model and a device: on a C-arm it never trained on, its vertebra detector finds 80.5\pct{} of
vertebrae (90.6\pct{} at a lower confidence threshold), against the 97\pct{} set in advance. Closing the gap takes
hundreds of GPU experiments on a shared computing cluster, which a person now writes, submits and repairs by hand,
and which the medical trainees who hold the clinical questions cannot write at all. Aim 1 builds and benchmarks an AI
agent that writes, submits and repairs its own cluster jobs and accepts a result only when a pass/fail test fixed
before the run passes; we expect it to finish at least 80\pct{} of 30 unseen tasks without human help, against under
half for the same model working in one shot. Aim 2 gives the agent to eight medical trainees and measures, task
against matched task, what it adds to the setup of the 2026 program. Aim 3 spends the agent's throughput on the
device gap, with learned X-ray simulation, and tests the result on CT-paired cadaver images taken at WSU.
Deliverables: the agent and its benchmark (MIT license, with the tasks a disclosure review clears), the cadaver set,
an FDA Pre-Submission, and NSF CAREER and NIH R21 proposals.

\section*{Project narrative}

\subsection*{1. Significance and innovation}
Surgeons confirm the level of a spine operation by counting vertebrae on an X-ray from a C-arm, the operating room's
mobile fluoroscope, and the count fails often enough to matter: a 27-hospital Japanese registry of 34,544 degenerative
spine operations recorded wrong-level surgery in 0.12\pct{} of them (0.42\pct{} in the thoracic spine) [1], and about
half of responding US spine surgeons report at least one wrong-level operation [2,3]. It fails most on unusual
anatomy: 12.9\pct{} of 550 surgical patients had a lumbosacral transitional vertebra, and their levels were
miscounted in clinic in 54\pct{} of them [4]. The best-studied aid, LevelCheck, registers the patient's preoperative
CT to each image [5] in 17 to 72\,s in an operating room [6], inside or at the edge of the 20\,s to one minute that
surgeons said they would wait [7].

The Open Spine Consortium at Wayne State has built the parts of a check that reads the C-arm image alone:
CTSpinoPelvic1K, an open dataset of 802 CTs with every vertebra, the sacrum and the hips labeled [8]; a physics
renderer that turns them into C-arm images; and a detector and level model (Figure~3a) that, with no C-arm image in
training, name the level of 96.4\pct{} of the lateral vertebrae they detect on a public C-arm dataset (Figure~4a).
The detector does not yet carry to a new C-arm: on a device held out of its training it finds 80.5\pct{} of
vertebrae (90.6\pct{} at a lower threshold), short of the 97\pct{} set in advance (Figure~4b). Closing that gap takes
hundreds of GPU jobs written, refused and repaired on a shared cluster (one figure of its C-arm study took 250
fine-tunes), and the consortium's 2026 trainee program hit the same wall, its success resting on mentors expert in
Python, imaging software and Slurm [9].

Our premise is that the bottleneck has moved from the model to the experiments, and that an agent can run them if it
treats the cluster's refusals as errors to repair and accepts no result until a test fixed in advance passes. Three
aims test it (Figure~1). \aim{Aim 1} builds an agent that writes, submits and repairs its own jobs on the WSU grid;
its methodological contribution is a \emph{gate-conditioned repair loop that treats the scheduler as part of the
runtime}: cluster refusals (a wrong quality-of-service setting, a job killed for memory) and code errors enter one
loop that edits and resubmits the job, and the loop stops not at a clean run but when the job's gate passes, a gate
being a pass/fail test written and hashed before the first job, which the agent can read but not change. Agent benchmarks score code edits (SWE-bench [10]) or machine-learning
engineering on a dedicated machine (MLE-bench [11], RE-Bench [12]); none we know of scores an agent against a shared
scheduler's refusals and queue, under a fixed GPU budget, with gates fixed in advance. \aim{Aim 2} puts the agent in
medical trainees' hands through Beethoven, the consortium's browser extension (Figure~2), and \aim{Aim 3} turns its
throughput on the transfer problem, with a differentiable 3-D Gaussian X-ray renderer [13,14] built on the PI's
Gaussian-splatting work and a paired CT and C-arm cadaver set imaged at WSU.

\subsection*{2. Aims, approach and preliminary work}
\aim{Aim 1 (Foundational AI): a self-repairing agent for the grid.} The agent is a large language model (Claude
Code at a pinned model version) in a model-agnostic harness, working with no intervention (no human edit or
resubmission). \emph{Approach.} The benchmark holds 40 tasks from Aim 3's experiments and the consortium's task lists
(render banks, detector fine-tunes, figure rebuilds), each with a gate shown to pass on a reference solution and to
fail on a planted error: 10 for development and 30 sealed until the final run. Three arms share one model and one
budget per task (six repairs, two GPU-hours, one queued job at a time): one shot; a repair loop that stops at a clean
exit; and the gated loop, which stops only when the gate passes. Each arm runs every task three times (360 runs),
pass rates reported by task type. \emph{Preliminary work.} The consortium's workers already run as Slurm
jobs through Beethoven, and our skeleton runs the loop against a simulated scheduler with the grid's
partitions and refusals, a design check and not a result. \emph{H1:} the gated loop passes at least 80\pct{} of the
30 sealed tasks with no intervention and no rule violation, against under half in one shot, and more than the repair
loop, whose clean exits that fail their gate are false successes.

\aim{Aim 2 (Education and Workforce): medical trainees drive the agent.} \emph{Approach.} Eight medical students and
residents in two waves of four \tbd{confirm} each receive a grid account, Beethoven and a gated sub-project of their own
or from Aim 3's list; the agent writes and repairs the jobs, and the trainee owns the question, the result and the
pull request a mentor reviews. In week 1 each trainee does two matched starter tasks from the
10 development tasks, never a sealed one, one with the agent and one with the 2026 setup (Claude as a coding
assistant, the trainee submitting the jobs), in counterbalanced order. The 2026 comfort instrument is given before
and after each wave, and an IRB determination is requested in month 1, as in 2026.
\emph{Preliminary work} [9]: in 2026, 16 students, seven with no coding and eleven with no imaging experience, worked
on ten sub-projects with Claude as a coding assistant and mentors expert in Slurm; all six domains improved on
retrospective before-and-after ratings (Figure~2c), and all ten sub-projects went to a national meeting as
abstracts. The report names what it lacked: a control group and objective measures. \emph{H2:} with
the agent, trainees pass more starter gates without a mentor's edit and reach their first passing GPU job sooner;
with eight trainees this is an estimate with 95\pct{} intervals that sizes the CyberTraining Pilot; comfort is
secondary.

\aim{Aim 3 (AI for Health): labeled 3-D structure into 2-D fluoroscopy, at low latency.} \emph{Data.} Physics renders
of CTSpinoPelvic1K, VerSe and TotalSegmentator CTs (7,045 standard and 1,482 transitional annotated frames) and a
device-styled corpus of 142 TotalSegmentator spines; real frames of three devices: C-ArmLSpine (149 public lumbar
cases, each a cone-beam CT with AP and lateral frames, truth automatic: two CT labelers agreeing), DeepFluoro
(cadaver pelvis fluoroscopy) and COBRA (Elekta radiotherapy imaging). \emph{Model} (Figure~3a): a detector finds each
body and the sacrum, a level model names each body, a count up from the sacrum checks the names, and the stack
abstains where count and shape disagree. New here, the cross-device detector learns each body's four corners,
labeled for free by projecting the CT body's corners at the device's geometry (Figure~3b), as the consortium's film
detector already does. \emph{Preliminary results} (Figure~4). With no C-arm image in training,
the level model names 96.4\pct{} of the 617 lateral bodies its detector finds on C-ArmLSpine (88.0\pct{} of all 676
with agreed truth) but 43.7\pct{} of the 631 AP bodies; fine-tuned on the AP frames of five cases it names
90.8\pct{}, and 98.1\pct{} with all cases, each test case held out. On the 233 frames where the sacrum is found
(81.5\pct{} of the 286 that show one), checking each name against the count names 98.2\pct{} of 1,009 bodies, and
the stack abstains on 9.9\pct{} of those frames, catching five of the six it would have named wrongly. Detection
does not carry: trained on two other devices' real frames, the detector finds 80.5\pct{} of C-ArmLSpine's
vertebrae and 76.8\pct{} of its sacra at the cross-device threshold of 0.74 (90.6\pct{} and 83.9\pct{} at 0.25), and
93.9\pct{} of the 214 vertebrae whose truth is complete and at least 80\pct{} visible. \emph{Closing the gap.}
Today's renders hurt: added to real frames, styled renders lower detection from 80.5 to 63.1\pct{} at 0.74
(90.6 to 87.8\pct{} at 0.25), and a classifier tells them from real frames 99 to 100\pct{} of the time. Physics
simulation with strong domain randomization (DeepDRR [15]) has matched real-data training for hip landmarks in
cadaver X-rays (SyntheX [16]); we randomize devices' geometry and spectra likewise, hold out each device in turn, and
fit the Gaussian renderer to a subset of the labeled CTs, learning only spectrum, scatter and detector response until
that classifier fails, so the anatomy and its labels stay exact. The agent runs every Aim 3 job; GPU-hours and human
hours per passed gate are Aim 1's field measures. \emph{Cadaver set.} Two specimens from WSU's Body Bequest Program
are CT-scanned and imaged AP and lateral at lumbar and thoracic positions on WSU's C-arm (Room 0312 Elliman, through
DLAR); a frame grabber records the video output, the set is sealed by hash before any
model sees it, and registering each specimen's CT to its frames [5] projects the CT's labels onto every frame. The intended use is an advisory level label
on each C-arm image, which the surgeon confirms; end-to-end latency is unmeasured, and Aim 3 measures it first. \emph{H3:} on each held-out device the detector finds 97\pct{} of vertebrae and of sacra with no more false
detections than today's at 0.74; the stack names a frame in under 1\,s on a workstation GPU; and on every cadaver
frame it names the right level or abstains.

\subsection*{4. Expected outcomes, deliverables and timeline}
Within the award: the agent, benchmark and three-arm report; the cohort's outcomes; the cadaver set,
deposited beside CTSpinoPelvic1K as the Body Bequest Program's terms allow, and a transfer and latency manuscript;
an FDA Pre-Submission; and the CAREER and R21. The seed buys what does not exist yet:
the researcher's hours, the agents' seats, the cadaver set and regulatory review; the trainee program, grid, pipelines
and faculty time come in kind, and the Gaussian renderer is a feasibility fit, its full build left to
the R21. \emph{Quarter 1:} agent version 1, gates sealed, IRB determination, recruitment, bookings. \emph{Quarter 2:}
benchmark run, wave 1 (months 4--7), cadaver imaging, transfer and latency results, Pre-Submission. \emph{Quarter
3:} CAREER, wave 2 (months 8--11), the renderer's fit. \emph{Quarter 4:} R21, final report, symposium.
\emph{Risks.} An agent not ready by month 4 starts wave 1 on the 2026 setup; a
delayed specimen leaves the held-out devices as the test; a transfer result under 97\pct{} becomes the R21's first
aim; budgets cap each task's cost.

\item \textbf{A, A2.} A graduate student assistant paid hourly, the call's graduate student support, or else a
  post-baccalaureate researcher: ten hours a week for 52 weeks at \$15.00, Michigan's minimum wage from January 1,
  2027 (MCL 408.934) \tbd{WSU's job category and scale}. Temporary employees and students are not benefit-eligible,
  so A2 is FICA alone: 7.65\pct{} for a hire who is not an enrolled student, where a student assistant's composite

  half an hour; Aim 2's run the cohort's accounts and measures; Aim 3's are 100 curating the cadaver set

  against their gates. At \$18 an hour the total would be \$20,247, over the cap unless the hours fall to 507.
\item \textbf{B1 to B5.} No public dataset we know of pairs a diagnostic CT of the thoracolumbar spine with C-arm
  frames of the same body: C-ArmLSpine pairs lumbar cone-beam CTs with frames posed by edge registration, and
  DeepFluoro pairs cadaver CTs with fluoroscopy of the pelvis. The cadaver set adds WSU's own C-arm, read through the
  video output a device would read. The Body Bequest Program's donor authorization permits de-identified images
  \tbd{the program's terms for public release}. Each user of the C-arm enrolls in WSU's dosimetry program and files
  OEHS's training form, both with DLAR's director 15 business days before the session. The frame grabber records what
  the device shows, the deployment input \tbd{the C-arm's model and video output}; at \$619 it is far under WSU's
  \$5,000 capital threshold and becomes WSU property.
\item \textbf{C1, C2.} Anthropic's Team plan for scientists lets a verified PI add Premium seats at \$15 a month,

\item \textbf{E, F.} AI for Science awards up to \$20,000 in API credits for six months and charges nothing to apply;
