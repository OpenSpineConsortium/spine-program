\vspace{6pt}
{\bfseries Abstract.} Wrong-level spine surgery persists because surgeons confirm the level by counting vertebrae on an
X-ray from a C-arm, the operating room's mobile fluoroscope. The count fails in patients
with an extra or missing lumbar segment and on images that show too few vertebrae. The Open Spine Consortium at Wayne
State has built the parts of an automated check: 802 CTs with every vertebra labeled, a renderer that turns them into
simulated C-arm images, and a level model. Never trained on a C-arm image, it names the level of
96.4\pct{} of the lateral vertebrae a film-trained detector finds on a public C-arm dataset (88.0\pct{} counting
those it misses). Detection is the gap this project attacks first: a second detector, trained on two other devices'
frames, finds 80.5\pct{} of the vertebrae on that dataset's C-arm (90.6\pct{} at a lower confidence threshold),
against the 97\pct{} set in advance. Closing the gap takes
hundreds of GPU experiments on a shared computing cluster, which a person now writes, submits and repairs by hand,
and which the medical trainees who hold the clinical questions cannot write at all. Aim 1 builds and benchmarks an AI
agent that writes, submits and repairs its own cluster jobs and accepts a result only when a pass/fail test fixed before the run
passes. We expect it to finish at least 80\pct{} of 30 unseen tasks without human help, against under
half for the same model working in one shot. Aim 2 gives the agent to eight medical trainees and measures, task
against matched task, what it adds over a coding assistant, the 2026 program's setup. Their literature questions go to a language model on
the grid, which quotes Scopus abstracts verbatim, with citation and DOI, under Elsevier's text and data mining terms,
and no passage reaches an outside AI service. Aim 3 turns the agent on the
detection gap, with learned X-ray simulation, and tests the result on CT-paired cadaver images taken at WSU. For the
clinical validation after the award, an IRB protocol awaiting submission would add about 4,000 Detroit Medical Center
spine surgery patients' CTs, operative notes and radiology reports, with any transitional vertebra recorded.
Deliverables: the agent and its benchmark (MIT license, with the tasks an invention-disclosure review clears), the cadaver set,
an FDA Pre-Submission, and NSF CAREER and NIH R21 proposals.

\section*{Project narrative}

\subsection*{1. Significance and innovation}
Surgeons confirm the level of a spine operation by counting vertebrae on an X-ray from a C-arm, the operating room's mobile fluoroscope. The
count fails often enough to matter: a 27-hospital Japanese registry recorded wrong-level surgery in
0.12\pct{} of 34,544 patients having degenerative spine surgery (0.42\pct{} in the thoracic spine) [1],
and about half of responding US neurosurgeons report at least one wrong-level operation [2,3]. It fails most on a transitional vertebra, part lumbar
and part sacral: 12.9\pct{} of 550 surgical patients had one, and their levels were
miscounted in clinic in 54\pct{} of them [4]. The best-studied aid, LevelCheck, registers the patient's preoperative
CT to each image [5] in 17 to 72\,s in an operating room [6], inside or at the edge of the 20\,s to one minute
surgeons said they would wait [7].

The Open Spine Consortium at Wayne State has built the parts of a check that needs no CT, only the C-arm image:
CTSpinoPelvic1K, an open dataset of 802 CTs with every vertebra and the sacrum labeled [8]; a physics
renderer that turns them into C-arm images; and a level model (Figure~3a). With no
C-arm image in training, it names the level of 96.4\pct{} of the lateral vertebrae a film-trained detector finds on a public C-arm dataset
(Figure~4a). Detection does not yet carry to a new C-arm: on one held out of
its training, our C-arm detector finds 80.5\pct{} of vertebrae (90.6\pct{} at a lower threshold), short of the 97\pct{} set in advance
(Figure~4b). Closing that gap takes hundreds of GPU jobs written, refused and repaired on a shared cluster (one
results figure took 250 fine-tunes). The consortium's 2026 trainee program hit the same wall: it
relied on mentors expert in Python, imaging and cluster computing [9].

Our premise is that the bottleneck has moved from the model to the experiments, and that an agent can run them if it
treats the cluster's refusals as errors to repair and accepts no result until a test fixed in advance passes. Three
aims test it (Figure~1). \aim{Aim 1} builds an agent that writes, submits and repairs its own jobs on the WSU grid. Its
methodological contribution is a \emph{gate-conditioned repair loop that treats the scheduler as part of the runtime}. Cluster refusals
(a wrong quality-of-service, or QoS, setting; a job killed for memory) and code errors enter one loop that
edits and resubmits the job. The loop stops not at a clean run but when the job's gate
passes; a gate is a pass/fail test, sealed by hash before the first job, that the agent can read but not change. Agent benchmarks score code edits (SWE-bench [10]) or machine-learning
engineering on a dedicated machine (MLE-bench [11], RE-Bench [12]). None we know of scores an agent against a shared
scheduler's refusals and queue, under a fixed GPU budget, with gates fixed in advance. \aim{Aim 2} puts the agent in
medical trainees' hands through Beethoven, the consortium's browser extension (Figure~2). \aim{Aim 3} turns it on the detection gap, with a paired
CT and C-arm cadaver set imaged at WSU and a differentiable 3-D
Gaussian X-ray renderer, like those of [13,14], built on the PI's Gaussian-splatting work.

\subsection*{2. Aims, approach and preliminary work}
\aim{Aim 1 (Foundational AI): a self-repairing agent for the grid.} The agent is Claude Code, an AI coding agent,
at a pinned model version, run by a model-agnostic
test harness with no intervention (no human edit or resubmission). \emph{Approach.} The benchmark holds 40 tasks (10 for development, 30 sealed until the final run) from
Aim 3's experiments and the consortium's task lists (render banks, detector fine-tunes, figure rebuilds), each
with a gate shown to pass on a reference solution and to fail on a planted error. A hidden check on each sealed task, unseen by the agent, catches gamed gates. Three arms share one model and one
budget per task (six repairs, two GPU-hours, one queued job at a time): one shot, retried from scratch within the budget; a repair loop that stops at a clean
exit; and the gated loop, which stops only when the gate passes. Each arm runs each task three times
(360 runs). \emph{Preliminary work.} The consortium's agents run through Beethoven as jobs under Slurm, the
grid's scheduler; our prototype runs the loop on a simulated one
with the grid's partitions and refusals, a design check, not a result. \emph{H1:} the gated loop passes at least 80\pct{} of the
30 sealed tasks with no intervention, no rule violation and no failed hidden check, against under half in one shot, and more than fresh retries or the repair loop, whose
clean exits may fail their gate (false successes).

\aim{Aim 2 (Education and Workforce): medical trainees drive the agent.} \emph{Approach.} Eight medical students and
residents in two waves of four \tbd{confirm} each receive a grid account, Beethoven and a gated sub-project, their own
or from Aim 3's list. The agent writes and repairs the jobs; the trainee
owns the question and the result; a mentor reviews the code. Trainees' literature questions go to a language model on the grid
that quotes verbatim, with citation and DOI, from an index of spine-related Scopus abstracts, under the text and data
mining and AI rights Elsevier approved for WSU (full text awaits a ScienceDirect amendment); no passage
trains a model or reaches an outside AI service, Claude included; a misquoted passage fails a gate, and
the mentor marks any quotation that does not support the answer. In week 1 each trainee does two matched starter
tasks outside the agent's development and benchmark: one with the agent, one with the 2026 setup (Claude as a coding
assistant, the trainee submitting the jobs), task and order counterbalanced. The 2026 comfort survey is given before
and after each wave; an IRB determination is requested in month 1, as in 2026.
\emph{Preliminary work} [9]: in 2026, 16 students, seven with no coding and eleven with no imaging experience, worked
on ten sub-projects in that setup, with mentors expert in Slurm. All six domains improved on
retrospective before-and-after ratings (Figure~2c), and all ten sub-projects went to a national meeting as
abstracts. The report names what it lacked: a control group and objective measures. Aim 2 adds both. \emph{H2:} with
the agent, trainees pass more starter gates without a mentor's edit and reach each task's first passing GPU job sooner;
with eight trainees an estimate with 95\pct{} intervals that sizes the NSF CyberTraining proposal.

\aim{Aim 3 (AI for Health): labeled 3-D structure into 2-D fluoroscopy, at low latency.} \emph{Data.} Physics renders
of CTSpinoPelvic1K, VerSe and TotalSegmentator CTs (7,045 standard and 1,482 transitional annotated frames) and a
device-styled corpus of 142 TotalSegmentator spines; real frames of three devices: C-ArmLSpine (149 public lumbar
cases, each a cone-beam CT with AP (front-to-back) and lateral frames, truth automatic: two CT labelers agreeing), DeepFluoro
(cadaver pelvis fluoroscopy) and COBRA (Elekta radiotherapy imaging). \emph{Model} (Figure~3a): a detector finds each
body and the sacrum, a level model names each body, a count up from the sacrum checks the names, and the stack
abstains where count and shape disagree or the name is unsure. New here, the C-arm detector will learn each body's four corners,
as the film detector does, from labels made by projecting
the CT's corners at the device's geometry (Figure~3b), exact on renders. \emph{Preliminary results} (Figure~4). With no C-arm image in training,
the level model names 96.4\pct{} of the 617 lateral bodies the film-trained detector finds on C-ArmLSpine
(88.0\pct{} of all 676 with agreed truth) but 43.7\pct{} of the 631 AP bodies. Fine-tuned
on the AP frames of five cases, it names 90.8\pct{}, and 98.1\pct{} with all other cases, each test case held out. On the 233 frames where the sacrum is found
(81.5\pct{} of the 286 that show one), cross-validated models, checked against the count, name 98.2\pct{} of 1,009 bodies, and
the stack abstains on 9.9\pct{} of those frames, catching five of the six it would have named wrongly. Detection
does not carry: trained on two other devices' real frames, the C-arm detector finds 80.5\pct{} of C-ArmLSpine's
vertebrae and 76.8\pct{} of its sacra at 0.74, a cross-device threshold chosen on other devices (90.6\pct{} and 83.9\pct{} at 0.25); on truth
rebuilt after that scoring it finds 82.2\pct{} of the 287 and 93.9\pct{} of the 214 whose truth is complete and at least 80\pct{}
visible. \emph{Closing the gap.}
Today's renders hurt: added to real frames, styled renders lower detection from 80.5 to 63.1\pct{} at 0.74
(90.6 to 87.8\pct{} at 0.25), and a classifier tells them from real frames 99 to 100\pct{} of the time. Physics
simulation (DeepDRR [15]) with strong domain randomization has matched real-data training for hip landmarks in
cadaver X-rays (SyntheX [16]). We randomize devices' geometry and spectra likewise and hold
out each device in turn. We also fit the Gaussian renderer to a subset of the labeled CTs, learning only spectrum, scatter and sensor
response until that classifier is at chance, so the anatomy and its labels stay exact. The agent runs every Aim 3 job; GPU-hours and human
hours per passed gate are Aim 1's field measures. \emph{Cadaver set.} Two specimens from WSU's Body Bequest Program
are CT-scanned and imaged AP and lateral at lumbar and thoracic positions on WSU's C-arm.
A frame grabber records the video output, the set is sealed by hash before any
model sees it, and registering each specimen's CT to its frames [5] projects the CT's labels onto every frame. The intended use is an advisory level label
on each C-arm image, which the surgeon confirms. End-to-end latency is unmeasured, and Aim 3 measures it first. \emph{H3:} on each held-out C-arm the detector finds 97\pct{} of vertebrae and of sacra with no more false
detections than today's at 0.74; the stack names a frame in under 1\,s on a workstation GPU; and on every cadaver
frame it names the right level or abstains.

\subsection*{4. Expected outcomes, deliverables and timeline}
Within the award: the agent, benchmark and three-arm report; the cohort's outcomes; the cadaver set,
deposited beside CTSpinoPelvic1K as the Body Bequest Program's terms allow, and a transfer and latency manuscript;
an FDA Pre-Submission; and the CAREER and R21. The R21's clinical data come from the consortium's pending protocol
IRB-26-03-8648: about 4,000 DMC spine surgery patients over ten years, de-identified CT paired with operative notes and
radiology reports, built outside the award. The seed buys what does not exist yet:
the researcher's hours, the agents' subscriptions, the cadaver set and regulatory review. The trainee program, grid, pipelines and
faculty time come in kind; the Gaussian renderer is fitted only as a
feasibility test, its full build left to the R21. \emph{Quarter 1:} agent version 1, gates sealed, IRB determination, recruitment, bookings. \emph{Quarter 2:}
benchmark run, wave 1 (months 4--7), cadaver imaging, transfer and latency results, Pre-Submission. \emph{Quarter
3:} CAREER, wave 2 (months 8--11), the renderer's fit. \emph{Quarter 4:} R21, final report, symposium.
\emph{Risks.} If the agent is not ready by month 4, wave 1 starts on the 2026 setup and the crossover moves to wave 2; a
delayed specimen leaves the held-out devices as the test; a transfer result under 97\pct{} becomes the R21's first
aim; budgets cap each task's cost.
