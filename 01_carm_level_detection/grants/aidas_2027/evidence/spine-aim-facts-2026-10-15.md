## The whole imaging corpus and the data platform

The consortium's data platform keeps one source card for each of 211 imaging sources: 176 upstream releases, 7 of the project's own releases, 23 derived products, 2 output roots and 3 data requests drafted but not sent. 115 of these sources are on disk on the Wayne State grid. Its CT census lists 21,622 CT volumes from 21,603 persons, one row per CT with duplicates folded across collections. Beside it the platform holds 39,705 plain radiographs in its film corpora and public real X-ray sets with 3-D truth, among them C-ArmLSpine (149 sessions, 299 spot frames, 149 cone-beam CTs) and COBRA2026 (692 cases, 375,618 projections, 771 GB). The holding was moved onto a 20 TB Ceph volume batch by batch, each batch checked against its source, and opened to approved students. On 2026-10-08 the imaging folder alone held 5,333.691 GB in 4,579,361 files, 1,552,040 of them DICOM. Every item is keyed to a person across naming schemes, and a held-out rule enforced by checks CHK-1 to CHK-9 and an evaluation gate found and fixed two cross-naming leaks. Not done yet: the content-hash chain and the two near-duplicate checks have never run on the real corpus, the holding index is not built, and no human has ruled on the census's transitional calls.

## Data mining at scale

Beyond the 802 labeled CTs, the consortium has mined public CT at scale to find the patients on whom vertebral counting fails: a census lists 21,622 CTs from 21,603 persons, and TotalSegmentator had segmented all but one of the 5,094 tier-1 CTs and all but 851 of the 12,217 tier-2 CTs by 2026-09-29. Two automatic labellers chosen to fail differently feed one registry of 6,108 persons, which holds 146 persons with a sixth lumbar vertebra and 14 with a thirteenth thoracic vertebra; 103 and 6 of them rest on the source datasets' own expert reads, and the rest are machine candidates. The screens were checked against outside truth and caught their own plausible errors: on 70 externally labelled CTs the two labellers found 12 and 17 of 28 sixth lumbar vertebrae, and a 9.3 % thirteenth-vertebra rate on 367 chest CTs fell to 5 CTs once a rib had to sit on the named vertebra. None of the 446 rendered review cases has a human verdict yet, so human verification, not segmentation, is now the bottleneck. A protocol-frozen search for public spine imaging datasets identified 254,935 records (228,553 after de-duplication), which await human screening, and a structured search found no open spine fluoroscopy video with vertebra labels among the sources it confirmed.

## Progress on the C-arm level problem

The work on the C-arm level problem goes well past the 802 CTs. It holds real X-ray frames from a surgical C-arm (C-ArmLSpine: 149 cases, 299 spot frames, two 301-frame rotational spins), a cadaver C-arm (DeepFluoro, plus a 2,659-frame vendor deposit), radiotherapy imagers (COBRA2026: 692 cases and 375,618 projections, 808.615 GB), a phantom C-arm (Geneva, 116 frames) and a cadaver flat panel (Ljubljana, 18 frames), along with a 12,709-film detector corpus. The measured results the packet does not yet quote are these:
- Five-fold, case-grouped naming reaches 97.4 % of 617 lateral and 96.4 % of 631 AP vertebrae.
- Counting up from the detected sacrum is right on 97.0 % of 1,009 bodies.
- Adding 24 frames from a new C-arm raises vertebra detection on that device from 7.7 % to 84.0 %, or 95.6 % on the repaired denominator.
- Physics cards fitted to four imagers reproduce held-out real frames with R^2 from 0.679 to 0.974.

The published field is thin. The only registration-free naming result is 88.3 % on cadaver long-length tomosynthesis; the best C-arm naming is 73.3 % on 31 images and needs a preoperative radiograph; no FDA 510(k) names levels from 2-D fluoroscopy. Unaided surgeons misnamed the level on 30.4 % (median) of 62 hard intraoperative images, and 17 % of self-reported wrong-level operations led to legal action. Toward a hospital test, the work has identified two deployment routes with FDA precedents (a Siemens Cios OpenApp and an external box like K180038), frozen the sample size (35 error-free persons, 22 carriers) and the literature comparators, drafted three hospital protocols, and built a service and a website client. Still missing: no protocol has been submitted, the hospital's C-arm is not recorded, there is no capture path, no latency for the reported stack and no cine score. The files do not support a claim of billions saved: the only published total is $1.3 billion over 21 years for all surgical never events.

## Plain films, renders and the dataset line

Beyond the 802-person CTSpinoPelvic1K release, the program holds 39,705 plain films across five radiograph corpora: CSXA 4,963, BUU-LSPINE 7,200 films of 3,600 patients, NHANES II 17,073 and VinDr-SpineXR 10,466. Of these, 9,108 level-annotated films form the level bank in use, and a separate corner corpus marks 33,943 vertebrae on 5,401 films. Physics renders of three CT collections add 7,045 annotated frames, from 798 CTSpinoPelvic1K people, 1,121 TotalSegmentator subjects and 282 VerSe series, plus 1,482 frames of 81 transitional subjects; these are the only labelled L6 and T13 vertebrae at scale. Image-formation cards were fitted from real frames of six imaging devices. On held-out persons the film level encoder reaches 92.7 % (NHANES II), 96.9 % (BUU-LSPINE) and 99.8 % (CSXA) exact level, render pre-training adds 11.1 to 21.9 points when only five annotated persons per corpus are available, and a film-trained detector found 160 of 161 lumbar vertebrae on 17 unseen NHANES II spines. The public line is: the CTSpinoPelvic1K v11 release on Zenodo (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), whose spinopelvic angles match published series (PI 52.1 against 52.1 in Veilleux 2020); a NeurIPS 2026 poster; an arXiv preprint; the Apache-2.0 OpenSpineToolkit; and the public website with its level atlas. No file records download, view or citation counts, and the planned hospital radiograph study (amendment 47 to the DMC trauma-registry protocol) was drafted but its submission is not recorded.

## The cost of wrong-site and wrong-level surgery, checked on the open web

The published record supports a sharp, defensible cost sentence, but not "billions a year". Wrong-site surgery is a named serious reportable event on the National Quality Forum list, and Medicare has refused payment for it since January 15, 2009. Across all surgical never events in the US National Practitioner Data Bank, 9,744 paid claims totalled $1.3 billion over 1990-2010 (Mehtsun 2013). In England, 978 spinal-surgery negligence claims over five years were valued at 535.5 million pounds (Machin 2018). Litigated wrong-disc-level cases mostly ended in a payment (Goodkin 2004). The program's own evidence brief (09_wrong_level_clinical_qi/docs/WRONG_SITE_BURDEN_EVIDENCE.md) found no source for annual costs in the billions, because the $1.3 billion is a 21-year total for all never events.
