# Prior art: vertebral level identification by humans and by software

Deep-research run wf_93111768-3ea, 2026-09-17: 106 agents, five search angles, three-vote adversarial verification per claim. Only claims that survived are listed as findings.

## Summary

Across the 24 verified claims, exactly one number is a measured human exact-level naming accuracy on an intraoperative image: Manbachi 2018, where 5 surgeons naming the target vertebra on 62 deliberately hard intraoperative radiographs had a median unassisted error of 30.4% (about 70% correct), falling to 2.4% (about 97.6%) with LevelCheck overlay (p<0.01) on the same images. LevelCheck's own headline figures (Otake 2012 99.998%, Lo 2015 100%/99.3%, De Silva 2016 100%) are registration-success rates against a labelled preoperative CT, not naming accuracies, and every one requires a patient-specific labelled CT (or MRI) so they sit in a different column from a registration-free classifier; the only registration-free AI naming number in the set is Huang 2022 (Siewerdsen group MVMS network), 88.3% per-vertebra labelling on 100 cadaveric Long-Film images after training solely on DRRs synthesised from 297 CTs. The wrong-level-surgery literature (Mody 2008: 50% of surgeons ever, 1 in 3,110 procedures; Jhawar 2007: 12.8 per 10,000 lumbar discectomies after erratum; Mayer 2014: 68% of surgeons ever, cited range 0.32-15%; Ammerman 2006: 15% incorrect initial exposure in 100 consecutive discectomies) is a surgical-outcome baseline, self-reported except Ammerman, and cannot be placed on the per-image naming axis. No verified study excluded transitional or anomalous spines, but none stratified by them either: unusual/unconventional anatomy is named as a cause of error in Jhawar and Mayer, four transitional patients sat in Ammerman's cohort, and LevelCheck papers show T13/four-lumbar and lumbarised-sacrum cases, yet no anomaly-specific naming accuracy exists anywhere in the verified set. The requested radiologist LSTV-numbering studies, inter-reader agreement figures, and the 2-D/3-D AI labelling papers (Cina, VertXNet, Kim, Konya, BUU-LSPINE, Kwak, VerSe, VERIDAH, SpineNet, Btrfly, Payer) did not survive or were not reached in this round and remain to be tabulated.

## Verified findings

### 1. Human baseline, exact-level naming on intraoperative radiographs (Manbachi 2018, Ann Biomed Eng 46(10):1548-57, PMID 30051244, PMC9107073): 5 surgeons, 62 intraoperative C-arm radiographs (7 cervical, 32 thoracic, 23 lumbar) selected as the hardest cases from a prior 398-radiograph multireader set, median per-surgeon localisation error 30.4% (IQR 5.2%) without assistance. Task is binary exact-level agreement with a neuroradiologist-defined truth on preoperative CT, no within-one-level credit. This is a difficulty-enriched figure the authors themselves say is not indicative of clinical practice; surgeon experience is not reported; no CI.

*Confidence:* high · *vote:* 3-0 (two duplicate claims, both unanimous)

**Evidence.** Table row: Manbachi 2018 | fluoroscopy/C-arm radiograph | mixed cervical-thoracic-lumbar, single intraoperative field | n=62 images, 5 readers | exact level named | 30.4% median error (IQR 5.2%), i.e. ~69.6% correct | anomalies INCLUDED (cases enriched for lumbarised sacrum, thirteenth thoracic vertebra) but not stratified | no AI in this arm. Verbatim: 'The accuracy of surgeons' localization was analyzed as the fraction of cases for which their decision agreed with the true vertebral level'; 'These images were identified in the prior multireader study as the most challenging cases to localize'; 'Such a high rate is not indicative of clinical practice'. Median/IQR are over n=5 readers' per-surgeon fractions. Merged claims [7] and [12].

Sources: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30051244/> · <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9107073/>

### 2. AI-assisted vs unassisted comparison on the same readers and images (Manbachi 2018): LevelCheck overlay cut the median error from 30.4% to 2.4% in both workflow modes, Independent Check (labels after the decision; IQR 10.6%) and Active Assistant (labels before; IQR 3%), p<0.01 by paired t-test over the 5 surgeons; confidence increased in 91% (373/410), unchanged 5.8% (24/410), degraded 3% (13/410). A separate prospective clinical arm (2 surgeons, 20 patients, 8 cervical/4 thoracic/8 lumbar, mobile C-arm) had 0 discrepancies. LevelCheck is registration-based: it needs a preoperative CT (or MRI in the 2017 MR-LevelCheck variant) with levels pre-labelled and solves a 6-DOF 3D-2D pose; runtime 17-72 s. This is the only AI-assisted vs unassisted reader comparison in the verified set and it is not randomised.

*Confidence:* high · *vote:* 3-0 (four duplicate/overlapping claims, all unanimous)

**Evidence.** Table row: Manbachi 2018 assisted | same 62 images, same 5 readers | exact level | 2.4% median error (~97.6% correct) | anomalies included | REQUIRES preoperative labelled CT/MRI (registration). Verbatim: 'Median error rate was reduced to 2.4% (IQR = 10.6%) for localization with LevelCheck implemented as an Independent Check and to 2.4% (IQR = 3%) for implementation as an Active Assistant'; 'LevelCheck was found to increase confidence in 91% of all readings (373/410)'; 'the algorithm solves for the 3D orientation (pose) that relates a preoperative 3D image to an intraoperative 2D image'. Cautions: the 410-reading denominator does not reconcile with 5x62=310 plus 5 repeats per surgeon and should be quoted as reported; IQR 10.6% in Independent Check mode implies at least one surgeon stayed well above 2.4%; two Siemens Healthineers co-authors (Vogt, Kleinszig) and Siemens academic-industry funding. Merged claims [8], [9], [13], [14].

Sources: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30051244/> · <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9107073/>

### 3. LevelCheck algorithm accuracy lineage is geometric registration success given a labelled preoperative CT, never a naming accuracy, and must be tabulated in a separate column: Otake 2012 (Phys Med Biol 57:5485, PMC3429949) 99.998% (1 failure in 50,000 DRRs simulated from 10 TCIA CTs, mPD 0.2±0.2 mm, success = mPD<5 mm over five mid-thoracic targets, 4.7 s GPU) plus 35/35 real-fluoroscopy images of an anthropomorphic chest phantom (mPD 0.4±0.2 mm), no patients, no readers; Lo 2015 (Spine, PMID 25646750, PMC4433144) 100% at 200 multi-starts (~26 s) and 99.3% at 50 multi-starts (~7 s) over 10,000 trials = 500 random initialisations x 20 consecutive thoracolumbar patients (13 primary, 7 revision), one lateral mobile radiograph each, success = mPDE<5 mm, no CI, effective n=20; De Silva 2016 (Spine 41:E1249, PMID 27035579, PMC5045321) 100% of labels within the correct vertebral level on 398 intraoperative radiographs from 178 preoperative CTs (CT within one year), median PDE 6.13 mm (IQR 4.23), with success defined loosely as PDE<30 mm and manual initialisation plus a manual collimator mask.

*Confidence:* high · *vote:* 3-0 (seven claims, all unanimous)

**Evidence.** Otake verbatim: 'Trials were defined as successful if the estimated position was within the projection of the vertebral body (viz., mPD < 5mm). Simulation studies showed a success rate of 99.998% (1 failure in 50,000 trials)'; 'One limitation of the initial implementation is the requirement for preoperative CT data in which vertebral levels ... are defined'. Lo verbatim: 'At 200 robust multi-starts ... the registration accuracy was 100% across all 10,000 trials ... 99.3% registration accuracy at 50 multi-starts'; 'Five hundred random initializations were performed for each patient'; 'a successful registration was defined as one yielding mPDE< 5 mm'. De Silva verbatim: '100% geometric accuracy (i.e., overlaid labels within the correct vertebral level in all cases) ... PDE ... was 6.13 ± 4.23 mm ... successful registration (PDE <30 mm)'. Field-of-view caveat directly relevant to a C-arm classifier: De Silva's 398 are survivors of 504 radiographs, with 106 (21%) excluded including 13 for 'limited field-of-view covering 3 or fewer spine levels', 22 for minimal CT overlap, 53 cervical setup differences, 21 poor quality; so 100% is 100% of pre-screened radiographs that already showed 4+ levels. Readers 2 and 3 flagged 0.3% and 6.0% of registrations as questionable (label near vertebral periphery). Anomalies INCLUDED in all three (Lo: 13 rib pairs with four lumbar vertebrae, T13 as target, registered successfully at 200 multi-starts; De Silva lists T13, L6, lumbarised sacrum among dataset complexities and cites 15-25% LSTV prevalence). Merged claims [15], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22].

Sources: <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3429949/> · <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4433144/> · <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045321/>

### 4. De Silva 2016 measured no surgeon naming accuracy at all: its three spine surgeons answered a utility questionnaire, and the figures 42.2% helpful (168/398), 30.6% potentially improved confidence (122/398), 0/398 degraded confidence are Likert-style utility judgements. They must not be entered as either a human or an AI naming accuracy. The prior 398-case study therefore has no unassisted baseline, which is why Manbachi's 62-case subset is the only measured human number in the lineage.

*Confidence:* high · *vote:* 3-0

**Evidence.** Verbatim: 'surgeons were queried regarding: purpose of the radiograph; image quality; whether localization support would be helpful; and approximate permissible time for decision support'; 'LevelCheck was judged to be helpful in 42.2% of the cases (168/398) ... to potentially improve the surgeon's confidence in 30.6% (122/398) ... In no case did the algorithm degrade surgeon's confidence.' No question asked the surgeon to name a level and no answer was scored against truth. Claim [16].

Sources: <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045321/>

### 5. The only registration-free AI exact-level naming number in the verified set is Huang 2022 (Comput Methods Programs Biomed 227:107222, PMID 36370597, doi 10.1016/j.cmpb.2022.107222; Siewerdsen/Uneri group): the multi-view multi-slot (MVMS) network reached 88.3% labelling accuracy on 50 AP + 50 lateral Long-Film (long-length tomosynthesis, O-arm multi-slot collimator) images from 13 cadaveric specimens, with detection F1 >96% and mean localisation error 3.3 mm (those two are detection metrics, not naming), 88.9% vs 91.2% with vs without instrumentation, +15.5 and +35.0 points over Faster R-CNN on AP and lateral. It was trained entirely on images synthesised from 297 CTs and tested on real cadaver acquisitions with no CT of the test subject, i.e. a synthetic-to-real, registration-free result. No CI, no within-one-level metric, per-vertebra denominator inferred not stated, anomalous-spine status of the 13 cadavers not stated.

*Confidence:* high · *vote:* 3-0

**Evidence.** Table row: Huang 2022 | Long-Film tomosynthesis (not plain fluoroscopy) | long-length whole thoracolumbar field (~17 vertebrae per image) | 100 images, 13 cadavers, no human readers | exact level per vertebra | 88.3% | anomalies NOT STATED | no preoperative CT of the test subject (registration-free), trained on DRRs from 297 CTs. Verbatim abstract: 'The network was trained on images synthesized from 297 CT images and tested on 50 AP and 50 lateral Long-Film images acquired from 13 cadaveric specimens ... achieved an F1 score of >96% and an average vertebral localization error of 3.3 mm, with 88.3% labeling accuracy on both AP and lateral images'. Full text is paywalled (not in PMC; Elsevier/SPIE 403). This is the closest published comparator to a registration-free classifier and it bears directly on the SyntheX-style lead (DRR-only training transferring to real images), although it tests long-length fields, not limited C-arm windows. Claim [23].

Sources: <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmpb.2022.107222> · <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36370597/>

### 6. Wrong-level surgery incidence from surgeon self-report surveys is a career-prevalence or per-procedure outcome, not a per-image naming accuracy, and should be tabulated in a third column labelled correct-level-surgery: Mody 2008 (Spine 33:194, PMID 18197106; anonymous 30-question postal survey of 3,505 AANS members, 415 responses, 12%) 50% (207/415) had performed at least one wrong-level surgery, 15% (64) had prepared the wrong level and caught it before incision, 418 wrong-level operations over an estimated 1,300,000 procedures = 1 in 3,110 (0.032%; 71% lumbar, 21% cervical, 8% thoracic; 17% led to legal action), no CI. Jhawar 2007 (J Neurosurg Spine 7:467, PMID 17977186; anonymous survey of Canadian neurosurgeons, 75% response) 12.8 wrong-level events per 10,000 lumbar discectomies (6 events/4,695), 7.6 per 10,000 cervical, 2.0 per 10,000 craniotomies, all corrected by a July 2008 erratum (J Neurosurg Spine 9(1):109) from the widely re-cited pre-erratum 4.5/6.8/2.2. Mayer 2014 (Spine J 14:741, PMID 24012429; anonymous online survey of 2,338 NASS members, 173 responses, 7.4%, 72% orthopaedic, 28% neurosurgeons, no pilot or validation) 68% had localised the wrong thoracic or lumbar level at least once, some corrected intraoperatively; fluoroscopy was the most-used localisation method (89% thoracic, 86% lumbar), plain radiographs 54%/58%; at the time of the error 56% had used plain radiographs and 44% fluoroscopy; its background cites a literature range of 0.32% to 15% wrong-level exposure, which is not measured in the survey.

*Confidence:* high · *vote:* 3-0 (five claims, all unanimous)

**Evidence.** Mody verbatim: 'Two hundred seven (50%) reported that they had done 1 or more wrong level surgeries during their career'; 'From an estimated 1,300,000 spine procedures, 418 wrong level spine operations had been performed, with a prevalence of 1 in 3110 procedures.' Jhawar verbatim: 'the incidence of wrong-level lumbar surgery was estimated to be 12.8 [corrected] occurrences per 10,000 operations'; erratum existence confirmed via CrossRef (doi 10.3171/spi/2008/8/7/109) and the integer-event arithmetic check (4,695 x 12.8/10,000 = 6.0). Mayer verbatim: 'Sixty-eight percent of surgeons admitted to wrong-level localization, some of which were rectified intraoperatively, during their careers'; 'Wrong-level exposure is documented in 0.32% to 15% of cases.' Note the discrepancy in magnitude between the two per-procedure estimates: Jhawar's counted-volume lumbar rate (~1 in 781) is 4x Mody's recalled-volume rate (1 in 3,110); both are lower-bound, recall-biased. Longo 2012 (PMID 22484768) claim of 'almost 50%' wrong-level lumbar plus >10% wrong-side was REFUTED 1-2 and should not be cited from this round. Merged claims [0], [1], [2], [4], [5].

Sources: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18197106/> · <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17977186/> · <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24012429/>

### 7. The one prospectively measured intraoperative human localisation error (not self-report) is Ammerman 2006 (Surg Neurol 66:470, PMID 17084188): in 100 consecutive single-surgeon lumbar discectomies the surgeon's identification of the exposed level, checked against intraoperative x-ray, matched the intended level in 85% of cases (15% incorrect initial exposure), no CI; age and pathology level above L5-S1 predicted mismatch (P<.05). Four of the 100 patients had transitional vertebrae and were retained, but the abstract does not say whether they were among the 15 errors. This is a correct-level-exposure outcome judged at the wound, not an image-naming test, and 15% is the extreme high end of published exposure-error rates (Dablouk 2019: 13/301 = 4.3% with the same definition; Irace 2010: 1/818 = 0.12%), so it must not stand in as a representative surgeon baseline.

*Confidence:* high · *vote:* 3-0 (two claims, unanimous)

**Evidence.** Table row: Ammerman 2006 | intraoperative x-ray confirmation of surgeon's wound-level judgement | lumbar, single level | n=100 patients, 1 surgeon | correct-level exposure (not naming) | 85% correct / 15% wrong | anomalies INCLUDED (4 transitional) | no AI. Verbatim: 'After exposure, the surgeon was asked to identify the level exposed, which was confirmed by intraoperative x-ray. ... Four patients had transitional vertebrae. The intended level was initially exposed in 85% of cases.' Mayer 2014's cited upper bound of 15% is this study. The word 'unaided' is an inference; the abstract does not state whether pre-incision localising fluoroscopy was used. Merged claims [10], [11].

Sources: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17084188/>

### 8. No verified study excluded transitional or anomalous spines, but none stratified by them, so there is no published anomaly-specific naming accuracy for surgeons or for AI in this set. Anomalous anatomy is named as an error driver: Jhawar 2007 lists 'unusual patient anatomy and a failure to verify the operative site by radiography' as common contributors to incorrect-site spine surgery; Mayer 2014 lists failure to 'recognize unconventional spinal anatomy' among preoperative causes (with failure to visualise reference points and large body habitus) and poor communication, failure to relocalise after exposure and poor counting methods among intraoperative causes; Ammerman retained 4 transitional patients; Lo 2015 shows a 13-rib-pair/four-lumbar patient with T13 as target and states success 'even when there were anomalous anatomical variations, including either fewer or extra lumbar or thoracic vertebrae'; Manbachi 2018 enriched its lab set for lumbarised sacrum and thirteenth thoracic vertebra; De Silva 2016 cites 15-25% LSTV prevalence and lists T13/L6/lumbarised sacrum among dataset complexities. The paper's own term in Mayer is 'unconventional spinal anatomy', which is broader than transitional vertebrae; write it as 'includes transitional vertebrae', not 'i.e.'.

*Confidence:* high · *vote:* 3-0 (claim [3] verifier confidence medium because the Jhawar full text could not be read)

**Evidence.** Jhawar verbatim: 'For spine surgery, in particular, unusual patient anatomy and a failure to verify the operative site by radiography were also commonly reported contributors.' Mayer verbatim: 'Common sources of preoperative errors included failure to visualize known reference points, recognize unconventional spinal anatomy, and adequately visualize the level because of large body habitus.' Lo verbatim: 'Anatomical variant in which the patient had 13 pairs of ribs and four lumbar vertebrae, with T13 being a level of surgical interest'; 'anatomical variations such a sixth or transitional lumbar vertebra'. The Jhawar full text is paywalled, so 'did not stratify by LSTV' was verified only indirectly through five citing reviews that extract no anomaly-specific rate from it; the Cureus 2020 lumbosacral-variant review instead cites Wigh's series (5 of 42 transitional-vertebra patients operated at the wrong level from nomenclature error) for an LSTV-specific figure, which was not itself verified this round. Merged claims [3], [6] with the anomaly notes from [11], [14], [15], [19], [22].
