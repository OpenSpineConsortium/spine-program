## Read this first

1. **The "~90 % fluoroscopy plateau versus ~99 % radiograph parity" is not in the literature. It is this project's
   own result**, restated by the review from whatever prompt produced it (Table 3: 96.4 %, 595 of 617, on the
   C-ArmLSpine laterals; the film-bank figures near 99 %). It may be stated as our finding, never with a citation.
2. **"Up to 37 % of malplacements missed on 2-D fluoroscopy" is 100 − 63.** The X23D paper's introduction says plain
   X-ray detection of screw malplacement has "low accuracy (ranging from 63 % to 83 %)" and cites three cadaver
   studies of static radiographs. Learch 2004 is the 63 %: two readers classified 30 cadaver screws as in or out of
   the pedicle and were right for 63 % (CT 87 %). That is misclassification in both directions, on radiographs, not
   malplacements missed on fluoroscopy. Ferrick 1997 gives 73–83 %; Choma 2006 gives sensitivity 70.1 % and
   specificity 83.0 % on 120 screws (44 misplaced).
3. **LevelCheck's periodicity failure is real and characterised, but the review's "catastrophic misalignment in
   real-world clinical applications" is contradicted by the clinical series the manuscript already cites.** Otake
   2012: one failure in 50,000 simulated trials at the chosen population size (mPD < 5 mm), 47 failures in total
   across λ = 30–150, every one "a translational shift in the superior-inferior direction by one vertebral height".
   Lo 2015: 100 % at 200 multistarts (99.3 % at 50). De Silva 2016 (Spine): 100 % geometric accuracy. Manbachi 2018:
   20 of 20, no wrong-level localisation. De Silva 2016 (Phys Med Biol) is the paper that measured content mismatch
   (instruments, deformation): gradient correlation failed in > 14 % of cases and gradient orientation in 0 %.
4. **Pairwise Domain Adaptation is the one verified, peer-reviewed number for how much paired real data closes a
   synthetic-to-real gap on spine X-ray:** one million synthetic images for pretraining, 140 real images for
   adaptation, and the failure rate (TRE > 10 mm) fell from 16.07 % to 11.20 % on 420 real X-rays of 42 C-arm CT
   volumes. It is pose regression of a vertebra, not level naming, and an 11 % failure rate remained. The review's
   "generalize seamlessly … beyond the 90 % threshold" is not in the paper.
5. **None of the sources offered as a cure for level jumping tests level jumping.** The multi-view paper registers a
   pelvis (DeepFluoro, six specimens). Known-component registration is screw-placement quality assurance. DiffPose
   and XVR register a pelvis, a femur and cerebral vessels. The claim that a patient-specific network "circumvents
   the periodicity failure mode because it has memorised the subtle geometric variations between adjacent
   vertebrae" has no source at all.

## What the review gets right that the paper can use without a citation

- The acquisition-space vector (primary and secondary positioner angles, detector rotation, SID, source-to-isocenter
  distance, magnification, patient position) is a fair description of what a C-arm exposure varies, and the DICOM tags
  that carry it are correct (rows 4–8). Our sweeps already sample an AP-to-lateral arc per device.
- "Coverage, not count" is a hypothesis this project can test on its own banks (the memory notes say NHANES, CTSP and
  the film banks were split independently and that the styled corpora are clean). It is not a literature claim.
- Level jumping by one vertebral height is the published failure mode of intensity registration (row 12), and the
  LevelCheck clinical papers show it was engineered away with multistart and gradient orientation (row 13). That is
  the right framing for why a morphology classifier is a different mechanism, not a better optimiser.
