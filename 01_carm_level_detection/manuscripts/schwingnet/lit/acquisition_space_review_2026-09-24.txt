1. The Clinical Imperative and the Domain Gap 
The transition from preoperative static volumetric imaging to dynamic, intraoperative 
two-dimensional (2D) fluoroscopy represents one of the most significant computational 
bottlenecks in modern image-guided spine surgery. Preoperative Computed Tomography (CT) 
provides high-fidelity, three-dimensional (3D) representations of patient anatomy, which are 
essential for precise surgical planning, particularly in procedures requiring sub-millimeter 
accuracy such as pedicle screw placement. However, intraoperative fluoroscopy serves as the 
primary modality for real-time surgical execution, fundamentally stripping away critical depth 
information due to its projective nature. The reliance on planar X-ray imagery to evaluate 
complex 3D spinal anatomy frequently leads to anatomical misinterpretations; clinical 
evaluations demonstrate that the visual detection of pedicle screw breaches based solely on 2D 
fluoroscopy is highly subjective and yields low accuracy rates, sometimes failing to detect up to 
37% of malplacements. 
To mitigate the risks of neurological injury and subsequent revision surgeries caused by 
hardware malplacement, computer-assisted surgical navigation systems employ 2D/3D 
registration. This process computationally aligns the preoperative 3D CT volume with the 
intraoperative 2D fluoroscopic coordinate system, overlaying the planned trajectories onto the 
live surgical field. Deep learning architectures designed to automate vertebral body detection, 
segmentation, and pose estimation have shown immense theoretical promise. Yet, a pervasive 
analytical paradox persists: these convolutional models frequently reach a performance plateau 
at approximately 90% accuracy in the fluoroscopic domain, while simultaneously achieving 99% 
parity on highly standardized lateral or anterior-posterior (AP) radiographs. 
This performance gap is deeply rooted in two fundamental challenges. First, there is a 
pronounced domain shift between the synthetic Digitally Reconstructed Radiographs (DRRs) 
used to train these models and the physical realities of clinical fluoroscopy. Second, the 
fluoroscopic acquisition space possesses a vastly higher dimensionality than standard 
radiography, rendering traditional uniform dataset scaling inefficient and largely obsolete. 
Overcoming these barriers requires a departure from traditional deep learning dataset 
expansion, moving toward physics-informed differentiable rendering, coverage-driven sampling, 
and patient-specific self-supervised optimization. 
2. Decoupling Sample Count from Generalization: The 
Acquisition-Space Diversity Framework 
A central query in modern surgical computer vision is determining whether the injection of more 

training data can elevate saturated models from the 90% accuracy plateau to the 99% parity 
observed in lateral X-rays. The conventional approach to deep learning assumes that expanding 
the training dataset will proportionally and continuously increase generalization capabilities. 
However, in the context of mobile C-arm fluoroscopy, this assumption is structurally flawed. 
2.1 The Dimensionality of the Fluoroscopic Domain 
Standardized lateral and AP radiographs possess a highly constrained geometric variance. The 
acquisition space is inherently low-dimensional because variables such as the Source-to-Image 
Distance (SID), patient positioning, and equipment angle are rigidly controlled by strict 
radiological protocols. Consequently, the sampling density for standard radiography saturates 
quickly, and the underlying data distribution is easily mapped by a convolutional network, 
allowing for rapid convergence to 99% accuracy. 
Intraoperative fluoroscopy, acquired via highly maneuverable mobile C-arms, exists within a 
radically different geometric regime. C-arms are subject to extreme variance across multiple 
continuous spatial and procedural dimensions. The acquisition space of a single fluoroscopic 
image observation, x_i, must be represented as a multidimensional vector: 
x_i = [\theta_p, \theta_s, \theta_d, SID, ISO, M, P_{patient}, T_{table}, A_{anatomy}, C_{clinical}, 
E_{equipment}] 
In this formulation, \theta_p and \theta_s represent the positioner primary and secondary angles 
(e.g., right anterior oblique/left anterior oblique [RAO/LAO] and cranial/caudal [CRA/CAU] 
rotations). The variable \theta_d dictates detector rotation, SID specifies the Source-to-Image 
Distance, ISO represents the Source-to-Isocenter Distance, and M calculates the isocenter 
magnification (M_{iso} = SID/ISO). 
When a model evaluating fluoroscopic data reaches a 90% accuracy plateau, it is rarely 
indicative of a model-capacity limitation or label noise. Instead, this plateau signifies 
sampling-density saturation within a highly populated, redundant region of the clinical 
acquisition space. Injecting tens of thousands of randomly sampled clinical images into the 
training pipeline yields diminishing returns because routine intraoperative data heavily 
overrepresents standard AP and strict lateral views. Meanwhile, complex oblique trajectories, 
rotational views, and unique magnification states remain drastically underrepresented, forming 
"holes" in the acquisition space. 
2.2 Coverage-Driven Sampling and Entropy Metrics 
To breach the 90% threshold, the analytical paradigm must shift from random dataset scaling to 
targeted, coverage-driven sampling. Model performance must be evaluated not purely as a 
function of raw sample count, but as a function of acquisition-space coverage. 
Coverage (C_d) for a specific geometric dimension d is defined as the ratio of occupied bins to 
the total clinically relevant bins within that dimension. To quantify the distributional diversity of 
the entire dataset, a normalized entropy measure is applied: 
H_d^* = \frac{-\sum p_i \log p_i}{\log K} 
By parsing the metadata of a fluoroscopic corpus—specifically utilizing Digital Imaging and 
Communications in Medicine (DICOM) tags—data engineers can construct a projection-space 
density map.

3. Bridging the Synthetic-to-Real Domain Gap 
The extreme scarcity of accurately annotated, large-scale clinical fluoroscopy datasets 
necessitates the use of in-silico training pipelines. Ground-truth camera pose annotations and 
dense semantic segmentations are highly difficult to acquire in an active operating room due to 
workflow disruptions, radiation exposure constraints, and the manual labor required for 
sub-millimeter annotation. Consequently, models are trained on synthetic DRRs generated by 
projecting preoperative CT volumes into 2D space. 
However, standard DRR generation relies on simplistic ray-casting algorithms that merely 
integrate Hounsfield Units (HU) along a view-ray. These analytical simplifications fail to capture 
the complex physical realities of clinical fluoroscopy, which is characterized by non-linear X-ray 
attenuation, quantum noise, electronic readout noise, beam hardening, and anisotropic photon 
scattering. When a model trained on clean, analytical DRRs is deployed on real fluoroscopy, a 
massive domain shift occurs, leading to catastrophic performance degradation. 
3.1 Physics-Informed Rendering: DeepDRR and DiffDRR 
To bridge this synthetic-to-real gap, recent computational frameworks have augmented the DRR 

rendering pipeline with complex physics-based mechanics. Two industry-leading frameworks, 
DeepDRR and DiffDRR, represent divergent but equally critical approaches to this problem. 
DeepDRR utilizes a hybrid architecture, combining analytical forward projection with machine 
learning to simulate realistic X-ray image formation. The pipeline initiates a 3D material 
decomposition of the CT volume using a convolutional segmentation network, categorizing 
individual voxels into distinct physical classes such as air, soft tissue, and varying densities of 
bone. It subsequently applies a material- and spectrum-aware forward projector, injects specific 
quantum and electronic readout noise models, and incorporates a neural network-based 
Rayleigh scatter estimation. By estimating photon scatter in 2D rather than relying on 
computationally prohibitive 3D Monte Carlo photon transport simulations (which trace the 
probabilistic interactions of millions of individual photons), DeepDRR achieves exceptional 
clinical realism while maintaining acceptable generation speeds. 
Conversely, DiffDRR prioritizes interoperability with deep learning optimization architectures by 
rendering the entire projection process fully differentiable. DiffDRR mathematically reformulates 
Siddon's exact ray-tracing algorithm as a series of vectorized tensor operations native to 
PyTorch. This auto-differentiability is profoundly important for 2D/3D registration: it allows a 
neural network to backpropagate the registration loss directly through the rendering process, 
calculating gradients with respect to the intrinsic and extrinsic imaging parameters. Utilizing 
GPU-accelerated tensor computation, DiffDRR achieves rendering speeds equivalent to highly 
optimized C++ frameworks while enabling continuous gradient descent for camera pose 
refinement. 
3.2 Pairwise Domain Adaptation (PDA) 
Even with advanced physics-based rendering, a residual domain shift persists due to 
patient-specific anatomical deformations (such as respiration and soft-tissue movement) and the 
presence of surgical instrumentation not captured in the preoperative CT. Generative style 
transfer and domain adaptation techniques are deployed to address this remaining discrepancy. 
Pairwise Domain Adaptation (PDA) represents a highly efficient strategy for medical image 
registration. Unlike standard statistical domain adaptation methods (e.g., Maximum Mean 
Discrepancy), which require massive, unpaired datasets from both the source (synthetic) and 
target (clinical) domains to function effectively, PDA operates on a highly constrained set of 
paired data. By directly measuring the domain distance on a small number of precisely paired 
DRR and real fluoroscopy images, a PDA module extracts robust domain-invariant features. 
In this architecture, the PDA module acts as a plug-and-play feature extractor inserted into the 
convolutional layers of a pre-trained segmentation network. Because the PDA module focuses 
exclusively on filtering out stylistic discrepancies—such as varying image contrast, background 
scatter profiles, and quantum noise artifacts—without altering the geometric spatial relationships 
of the anatomy, it aligns the feature spaces of the synthetic and real domains. This enables a 
model trained exclusively on infinite synthetic DRR datasets to generalize seamlessly to the 
clinical fluoroscopic domain, significantly elevating vertebral body detection accuracy beyond 
the 90% threshold. 
4. The Periodicity Failure Mode in Spinal Registration 
Beyond the acquisition-space dimensionality and the synthetic-to-real domain gap, spinal 
imaging presents a highly unique anatomical challenge known as structural periodicity, 

commonly referred to as "level jumping". Vertebral bodies, particularly across the thoracic and 
lumbar segments, possess highly similar morphological features and projection profiles. 
When an automated 2D/3D registration optimizer attempts to align a CT volume to a 
fluoroscopic image by maximizing an image similarity metric (such as Mutual Information or 
Gradient Correlation), the optimizer can easily fall into a false local optimum. The algorithm may 
mistakenly register the L3 vertebra on the 3D CT to the L4 vertebra on the 2D fluoroscopic 
image because their planar projections are nearly identical under certain C-arm angles. 
Simulation studies evaluating intensity-based 2D/3D registration systems (such as the 
LevelCheck system) frequently report success rates of 99.998% in ideal, simulated 
environments, defining success as a mean projection distance (mPD) of less than 5mm. 
However, in physical experiments and real-world clinical applications, the presence of quantum 
noise, X-ray scatter, surgical implants, soft-tissue resection, and limited fields-of-view severely 
exacerbate the periodicity failure mode, leading to catastrophic misalignment. 
4.1 Multi-View and Known-Component Anchoring 
Overcoming the level-jumping phenomenon requires computational anchoring mechanisms. 
Multi-view registration approaches mitigate this issue by tracking anatomical features across 
multiple varying perspectives simultaneously, mathematically constraining the pose estimation 
so that a false optimum in one view is heavily penalized by the geometric realities of the 
orthogonal view. 
Alternatively, Known-Component Registration (KC-Reg) utilizes highly distinct, radiopaque 
surgical instrumentation—such as K-wires or pedicle screws—as fixed reference points. By 
injecting the precise 3D Computer-Aided Design (CAD) models of these surgical devices into 
the optimization loop, the algorithm anchors the registration to the devices known to be present 
in the intraoperative field. Utilizing the Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy 
(CMA-ES) to maximize image-gradient similarity, KC-Reg prevents the optimizer from slipping to 
adjacent vertebral levels, ensuring the registration remains bounded within the correct 
anatomical segment. 
5. AI-Driven 3D Reconstruction from Sparse Views: 
The X23D Paradigm 
A transformative approach to bypassing complex 2D/3D registration entirely involves the direct, 
AI-driven generation of a 3D anatomical volume from a highly sparse set of 2D fluoroscopic 
images. This approach eliminates the reliance on a preoperative CT scan, aiming to construct 
the 3D surgical scene entirely from intraoperative data. 
The X23D algorithmic framework represents the vanguard of this computational strategy. 
Designed to intraoperatively estimate the 3D shape of a patient's lumbar vertebrae directly from 
sparse, multi-view X-ray data (typically just 3 to 4 projections), X23D utilizes a learned 
multi-view stereo machine approach. The neural network incorporates the exact X-ray 
calibration parameters (extrinsic and intrinsic matrices) directly into its architecture, allowing a 
priori knowledge of the spinal shape to be applied while preserving strict patient specificity. 
5.1 Training Pipelines and Ex-Vivo Validation 
The X23D network relies heavily on synthetic data generation to achieve spatial understanding. 

The model was trained and rigorously evaluated on 17,420 fluoroscopy images that were 
digitally reconstructed and sampled from the public CTSpine1K dataset. When evaluated 
against unseen data, the network achieved an 88% average F1 score and a 71% surface score, 
fundamentally outperforming counterpart state-of-the-art methods by 22% in surface score 
accuracy by effectively utilizing the camera calibration parameters. 
To validate the clinical viability of this AI-driven reconstruction, comprehensive ex-vivo studies 
were conducted. In a highly controlled interventional setting, five board-certified spine surgeons 
were tasked with placing pedicle screws across the L1-L5 levels of human torsi. The surgeons 
placed a total of 49 pedicle screws (24 using the X23D prototype and 25 using conventional 2D 
fluoroscopy as the control). 
The analytical results highlighted the immense potential and current limitations of sparse-view 
3D reconstruction. Execution time for each lumbar level showed no statistically significant 
difference, with the X23D group averaging 167 seconds compared to the control group's 156 
seconds. Crucially, the radiation dose was heavily reduced in the AI cohort, dropping to 33.26 
mGy compared to 49.5 mGy in the control group. However, the study recorded five hardware 
breaches in the X23D group (one of which was considered clinically significant, graded as a 
medial breach C), compared to six breaches in the control group (also featuring one clinically 
significant breach). While X23D demonstrates that 3D reconstruction from 3-4 images is 
computationally feasible with processing times of just 81.1 milliseconds per vertebra, further 
architectural refinement is required to achieve absolute parity with clinical-grade, CT-based 
navigation systems. 
6. Rotational Fluoroscopy: Hardware and "Around the 
World" Modalities 
The specific inquiry regarding "around the world" shots on fluoroscopy refers to orbital or 
rotational fluoroscopic acquisitions. In this modality, a motorized C-arm performs a continuous, 
automated 180-degree (or greater) sweep around the patient on the operating table, capturing 
hundreds of rapid, sequential 2D projection images. These dense 2D sequences are 
subsequently processed via filtered back-projection or iterative reconstruction algorithms into a 
high-fidelity 3D Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) volume for immediate 
intraoperative verification.

7. Landscape of Paired Datasets: Public Repositories 
and Data Use Agreements 
The development of highly robust vertebral detection and domain adaptation algorithms requires 
paired datasets containing high-resolution 3D volumes (preoperative CT or intraoperative 
CBCT) and their corresponding raw, uncompressed 2D fluoroscopic projections with known 
extrinsic camera poses. 
7.1 Publicly Available Datasets 
Despite the inherent difficulties in acquiring perfectly calibrated clinical data, several benchmark 
datasets are publicly available to researchers: 
1.​ DeepFluoro: Curated by Johns Hopkins University, DeepFluoro is widely acknowledged 
as the gold standard for benchmarking 2D/3D registration algorithms. The dataset 
comprises information from six cadaveric subjects, featuring one high-resolution 
preoperative pelvic CT scan per subject. Accompanying the CTs are intraoperative 
fluoroscopic X-ray images, ranging from 24 to 111 images per subject, yielding a total of 
366 real images. DeepFluoro provides highly accurate, ground-truth extrinsic camera 
matrices for every 2D image—obtained through rigorous fiducial-based optical 
tracking—as well as the exact intrinsic calibration matrix of the specific C-arm used. While 
strictly focused on the pelvis and femurs rather than the spine, the registration 
methodologies, loss functions, and domain adaptation strategies developed on 
DeepFluoro are universally transferable to spinal anatomy. 

2.​ Ljubljana Dataset: This clinical dataset contains paired 2D and 3D digital subtraction 
angiography (DSA) images from 10 patients undergoing neurovascular interventions at 
the University of Ljubljana. Like DeepFluoro, it provides essential ground-truth extrinsic 
and intrinsic matrices derived from surface fiducial markers. Although primarily cranial and 
vascular in nature, the Ljubljana dataset is routinely utilized to test the generalizability of 
2D/3D registration neural networks across highly disparate anatomical domains. 
3.​ SpineWeb: The SpineWeb archive is a specialized, multi-institutional repository focused 
explicitly on spinal imaging. Specific sub-cohorts within SpineWeb provide paired CT 
images and calibrated fluoroscopic sequences explicitly designed for evaluating 3D/2D 
registration, automated spinal measurement quantification (SMQ), and the tracking of 
intervertebral kinematics under physiological load. 
4.​ CTSpine1K: While not containing real fluoroscopy, CTSpine1K is a massive public 
dataset of 3D spinal CTs containing detailed segmentation masks. Models like X23D 
leverage CTSpine1K to digitally render tens of thousands of synthetic rotational 
fluoroscopic views, serving as the foundational training ground before transitioning to real 
clinical data via domain adaptation. 
7.2 Restricted Datasets and Data Use Agreements (DUAs) 
Accessing large-scale, clinical, intraoperative rotational sequences generally requires 
institutional collaboration via a Data Use Agreement (DUA). The raw 2D projections from 
systems like the O-arm or Cios Spin contain vast amounts of identifiable biometric and spatial 
data, heavily protected under HIPAA and GDPR regulations. 
Institutional research networks frequently harbor immense, highly curated datasets. For 
example, researchers at Henry Ford Health maintain comprehensive biplane dynamic X-ray and 
CT datasets used for highly precise, model-based tracking of cervical kinematics in spinal 
arthroplasty and fusion patients. These datasets track 6-Degrees-of-Freedom (6-DoF) vertebral 
motion (3 translations, 3 rotations) at extensive longitudinal time points (e.g., 2-year and 
6.5-year post-operative reviews). Accessing such databases necessitates navigating 
institutional review boards (IRB), validating the scientific merit of the study, and establishing 
formal DUAs to ensure data anonymization and secure processing protocols. 
8. Optimal Utilization of an N=1 Paired Dataset 
The theoretical scenario posits the possession of exactly one "around the world" rotational 
fluoroscopic sequence with a corresponding preoperative CT scan of the spine. Under 
traditional deep learning paradigms, an N=1 dataset is fundamentally insufficient for training a 
generalized model due to catastrophic overfitting. However, contemporary advances in 
self-supervised learning, differentiable rendering, and patient-specific network adaptation allow 
for immense computational utility to be extracted from a single paired dataset. 
By deploying algorithmic pipelines analogous to DiffPose and XVR, a single paired dataset can 
be engineered to achieve sub-millimeter 2D/3D registration accuracy. The optimal pipeline is 
structured in three consecutive phases: Patient-Specific Pretraining, Pairwise Domain 
Adaptation, and Intraoperative Differentiable Refinement. 
8.1 Phase 1: Patient-Specific Pretraining via Differentiable Rendering 

Instead of attempting to train a network on generic population data that fails to generalize 
across disparate anatomies, the pipeline begins by treating the single preoperative CT scan as 
an infinite, self-supervised data generator. 
Using a differentiable renderer like DiffDRR, the pipeline randomly samples tens of thousands of 
theoretical camera poses (extrinsic matrices) from a uniform distribution spanning the entire 
rotational acquisition space. For each mathematically sampled pose, a highly realistic synthetic 
DRR is rendered directly from the specific patient's CT volume. A convolutional neural network 
is then trained from scratch (or fine-tuned from a foundation model) on these synthetic images. 
The input to the network is the synthetic DRR; the output is the direct regression of the 6-DoF 
camera pose. 
Because the data is generated in-silico, the ground-truth pose is known perfectly, establishing a 
flawless self-supervised training loop. The network becomes a localized, "patient-specific" 
expert. It implicitly learns the exact morphological structure of the patient's spine, entirely 
circumventing the periodicity (level-jumping) failure mode because it has extensively memorized 
the subtle, patient-specific geometric variations between adjacent vertebrae. 
8.2 Phase 2: Mitigating Intrinsic Calibration Errors and Domain Shift 
The patient-specific CNN trained in Phase 1 is highly accurate on synthetic DRRs but will 
inevitably suffer a performance drop when exposed to the real rotational fluoroscopy sequence 
due to the domain gap. Furthermore, the single fluoroscopic shot may possess unknown or 
inaccurate intrinsic calibration parameters, a common issue in clinical datasets. 
Computational analysis demonstrates that moderate errors in intrinsic calibration can be 
effectively mitigated solely by re-optimizing the extrinsic parameters during registration. 
Simulation and real-world experiments on C-arm systems indicate that focal length errors of up 
to 500 pixels introduce a mean 3D reconstruction error of less than 0.2 mm, while principal point 
shifts of up to 200 pixels yield negligible increases in reprojection error once the extrinsic pose 
is re-optimized. This high intrinsic tolerance allows the pipeline to proceed even if the exact 
camera parameters of the N=1 dataset are slightly degraded. 
To bridge the visual domain gap, the user’s real rotational sequence is deployed. The user 
selects a sparse subset of images from the real rotational sequence where the approximate 
camera poses are manually aligned or known. A Pairwise Domain Adaptation (PDA) module is 
attached to the CNN, and the network is fed paired images: the real rotational fluoroscopy and 
the corresponding DiffDRR synthetic projection. The PDA module calculates a localized domain 
loss, forcing the feature maps of the convolutional layers to become invariant to the stylistic 
differences (noise, contrast, scatter) between the synthetic and real domains. The 
patient-specific network can now process the real intraoperative fluoroscopy images with the 
same accuracy as the pristine synthetic DRRs. 
8.3 Phase 3: Intraoperative Differentiable Refinement 
When the domain-adapted, patient-specific network predicts a camera pose from a real 
rotational fluoroscopy image, it serves only as a highly accurate initialization. To achieve 
absolute parity with the 99% accuracy seen in rigid radiography, a final optimization step is 
required. 
The predicted pose is passed into the DiffDRR renderer, which generates a real-time synthetic 
DRR. An image similarity metric—such as Gradient-Normalized Cross Correlation (Grad-NCC) 
or Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS)—is computed between the synthetic 

DRR and the real target fluoroscopy. Because the entire rendering pipeline is formulated as 
vectorized tensor operations in PyTorch, the error gradient flows directly backwards from the 
image similarity loss, through the rendering engine, and into the 6-DoF pose parameters. 
Advanced architectures, such as the LXPose framework, utilize a multi-stage refinement 
process operating on axis-angle rotations, achieving inference times of just 20 milliseconds to 
comply with the strict 40 ms time constraint required for true intraoperative deployment. 
Alternatively, the XVR framework utilizes a multi-scale optimization strategy and 
gradient-normalized cross-correlation loss to achieve extreme sub-millimeter accuracy, though it 
requires slightly longer optimization times (approximately 7 seconds). 
This hybrid combination of self-supervised, patient-specific initialization followed by 
differentiable rendering refinement entirely bypasses the traditional limitations of 2D/3D 
registration. The neural network provides an initialization well within the capture range of the 
optimizer, preventing convergence on local minima, while the differentiable renderer achieves 
flawless structural alignment. 
9. Conclusion 
To elevate vertebral body detection and 2D/3D registration models from a saturated plateau of 
90% accuracy to the clinical parity of 99% observed in standardized radiographs, the 
computational paradigm must shift definitively from uniform dataset expansion to 
physics-informed, coverage-driven optimization. Relying solely on randomly acquired clinical 
fluoroscopy leaves severe statistical voids within the multi-dimensional rotational acquisition 
space, inherently stalling neural network generalization and leaving models vulnerable to 
geometric variations. 
The integration of auto-differentiable rendering engines, such as DiffDRR, paired with Pairwise 
Domain Adaptation, permits the creation of virtually infinite, accurately labeled, domain-invariant 
synthetic data directly from preoperative CT volumes. For researchers navigating severely 
constrained data environments—such as the possession of a single paired CT and rotational 
fluoroscopic sequence—the deployment of patient-specific, self-supervised regression networks 
initialized prior to iterative test-time optimization represents the definitive state-of-the-art 
methodology. 
By systematically mapping the sparse acquisition dimensions of C-arm trajectories, leveraging 
the known components of surgical hardware to prevent periodic level jumping, and enforcing 
structural alignment through continuous gradient-based optimization, the inherent spatial 
ambiguities of the spinal column can be definitively resolved. This computational approach 
successfully bridges the domain gap, unlocking highly accurate, real-time volumetric navigation 
directly from projective 2D intraoperative data.
