## 1. Course Description

You open the MRI before the read comes back. That is the ordinary reality of advanced practice in spine and neurosurgery — the images land in the chart, the patient is in the room or on the phone, and the formal interpretation is hours or days away. In that interval, decisions get made about who is worked in today, who gets a surgeon's number, and who gets reassured.

**This session teaches two skills and nothing else.**

**First: recognition.** A repeatable search protocol for cervical and lumbar MRI, and the specific visual signature of each finding that changes management — what it looks like, where it hides, and what mimics it. Taught by scrolling through real studies in front of the room, with the audience making calls before the answer is shown.

**Second: conversion.** How to turn what you just recognized into a guideline-anchored recommendation. Every finding in this talk terminates in a decision box drawn from the AO Spine clinical practice guideline (2017) and its 2025 recommendations update — operate, offer, watch, or reassure — with the strength of evidence attached.

What this session deliberately is *not*: a survey of the literature, a tour of advanced imaging techniques, or a lecture on surgical technique. Participants leave with a protocol they can run tomorrow morning, a structured template to run it with, and a decision table that tells them what their findings mean.

**The organizing principle:** a finding matters when it changes the mJOA category, the trajectory, or the guideline bucket. Otherwise it is description.

---

## 2. Learning Objectives

At the conclusion of this session, participants will be able to:

1. **Execute** a systematic eight-step cervical and seven-step lumbar MRI search protocol without reference to the radiology report.
2. **Recognize** the findings that change management — CSF effacement, cord deformation, T2 hyperintensity, T1 hypointensity, cord atrophy, foraminal root compromise, paraspinal denervation edema, and abnormal conus position — and **distinguish** each from its common mimics.
3. **Grade** severity using the systems that transfer to non-radiologists: Muhle, Kang, Schizas, Lee, and Pfirrmann.
4. **Score** the mJOA reliably and place a patient into the correct AO Spine severity band.
5. **Apply** the 2017 AO Spine clinical practice guideline and 2025 recommendations update to convert a finding-plus-mJOA into an operate / offer / observe recommendation with its strength of evidence.
6. **Triage** urgency correctly, and **articulate the boundary** between findings an APP can act on and findings that must be deferred for formal interpretation.

## 4. Module Outline

### Module 1 — Why This Skill, and Where Its Limits Are

- The interval problem: images exist before interpretations do, and decisions happen in that window.
- **Base rates first, so nothing later is over-read:** 52% of asymptomatic adults have disc bulges, 27% have protrusions [17]; disc degeneration runs 37% at age 20 to 96% at age 80 [18]. Degeneration on MRI is as informative as grey hair until it explains *this* syndrome at *that* level.
- **The scope line, stated up front:** what an APP can reliably call (compression, level, CSF effacement, cord signal, Schizas grade, root compromise, conus level, study adequacy) versus what must always be deferred (tumor, infection, marrow replacement, intramedullary non-compressive lesions, anything extraspinal).
- **The safety rule:** your read exists to raise urgency, never to lower it. A degenerative-looking scan does not exclude ALS, MS, B12 deficiency, or cord tumor.

*Field Guide §1.1, §1.5, §2.12*

---

### Module 2 — Sequence Orientation in Four Minutes

- The one-line heuristic: CSF bright → T2 or STIR. Fat also bright → T2. Fat dark → STIR/fat-sat.
- What each sequence is *for*, in one column: T1 (anatomy, marrow, myelomalacia), T2 (CSF contrast, cord signal, rootlets), STIR/fat-sat (edema — marrow, ligament, **denervated muscle**), GRE (calcification, OPLL, hemorrhage), post-contrast (tumor, infection, scar vs. disc, acute denervation).
- **Study adequacy as a clinical skill:** no axial T2 through the level of interest, or no fat-sat when hunting denervation, is a finding in itself — and catching it early saves a wasted clinic visit.

*Field Guide §1.2*

---

### Module 3 — The Cervical Read (live demonstration, eight steps)

Run in order, in front of the room, on a real study. Order is front-loaded by urgency.

1. Sagittal T2 mid-line — scroll the whole cord. *Where does the bright CSF stripe disappear?*
2. Sagittal T2 — cord signal at those levels. Focal or diffuse?
3. **Sagittal T1, same levels** — is the cord dark? (The step everyone skips.)
4. Axial T2 at every flagged level — this is where compression is actually graded.
5. Alignment — lordotic, straight, kyphotic. Five seconds, approach-determining.
6. Soft vs. hard: disc versus osteophyte versus OPLL.
7. Foramina.
8. Everything you were not looking for.

**Audience exercise:** the room calls Steps 1–3 out loud before the answer is revealed.

*Field Guide §1.3*

---

### Module 4 — Recognition Drills: the cord findings

For each finding: **where to look → what you see → how to grade → what fools you → why it matters.**

| Finding | Grading | The mimic to defeat |
|---|---|---|
| **CSF effacement** | Muhle 0–3 | CSF pulsation artifact; volume averaging |
| **Cord deformation** | Compression ratio (ICC 0.80) | Oblique slice angle at a kyphotic apex |
| **T2 hyperintensity** | Kang 0–2; focal vs. diffuse; count levels | **Truncation/Gibbs artifact** — the single most common false-positive cord finding. Linear, uniform, *vanishes on axial* |
| **T1 hypointensity** | Kang grade 2 | Under-windowed images — compare to uninvolved cord in the same image |
| **Cord atrophy** | Caliber vs. uninvolved levels | Physiologic tapering below the cervical enlargement; a roomy-looking canal because the cord shrank |

**Prognostic weight, delivered as counseling language rather than data:**
- Multilevel T2 → lower recovery potential; focal T2 → poorer walking outcomes; single-level T2 → only weakly predictive [8,11,33]
- Post-op **resolution** of T2 predicts best outcomes (RR 72% vs. 47%) [12]
- **T1 hypointensity is the highest-value finding in the talk** — largely irreversible; reduced recovery ratio (*p* = 0.03) and lower likelihood of optimal outcome (*p* = 0.005) [8]; its post-op resolution does *not* improve outcomes [12]
- **Translation for the clinic:** T1 hypointensity does not mean "don't operate." It shifts the conversation from *restoring function* to *preventing further loss* — and APPs are usually the ones having that conversation.

*Field Guide §2.1–2.6*

---

### Module 5 — The Lumbar Read + Schizas Drill

Seven steps: conus → thecal sac caliber → **Schizas at each level** → foramina on parasagittal → lateral recess on axial → STIR for denervation → marrow/alignment.

**Schizas as the centerpiece.** The practical shortcut taught as a single question: ***can you still count individual rootlets?*** Yes → A or B. No → C or D.

| | |
|---|---|
| A1–A4 | CSF clearly visible; rootlet position varies |
| B | Rootlets fill the sac but remain individually distinguishable; grainy |
| C | No rootlets distinguishable; homogeneous grey; posterior epidural fat present |
| D | As C, **plus** no posterior epidural fat |

- **Why this one is worth teaching APPs:** Schizas and Lee both show excellent inter-observer agreement and are explicitly validated as **learnable by clinicians as well as radiologists**. [26]
- **What it predicts:** grades C and D are more likely to fail conservative treatment; A and B less likely to warrant surgery. [25]
- **What it does not predict:** in NORDSTEN, MRI findings had no clinically relevant association with baseline disability or pain. [27] Schizas predicts *conservative treatment failure*, not how much the patient hurts.

**Audience drill:** four axial images, graded live by the room.

*Field Guide §1.4, §2.7*

---

### Module 6 — Beyond the Canal

**6a. Nerve root compromise.** Describe what the disc does *to the nerve*: no contact → contact → compression. [32] Formalized as Pfirrmann 0–3 (normal / contact / deviation / compression) [34] and Lee 0–3 for foraminal stenosis. [35] Key concept: **perineural fat is your contrast agent** — grading asks how much of the fat halo is gone and whether the root itself is deformed. Pitfall: confusing the *exiting* root with the *traversing* root is a wrong-level error.

**6b. Paraspinal denervation edema.** The level localizer when a multilevel degenerative study will not say which root is symptomatic. Acute–subacute (~2 weeks): T2 fat-sat/STIR hyperintense, T1 iso-intense, enhances. Chronic: edema resolves, fatty infiltration and volume loss remain. **Side-to-side comparison is essential** — the finding is a difference. Stated pitfall: readers focused on the canal routinely miss it, and it is nearly invisible without fat-sat. [37] An APP who knows to look can request the sequence.

**6c. Conus level.** At or above mid-L2 is normal at any age; caudal to mid-L2 raises concern for tethering. [38] A five-second check on the sagittal T2, skipped constantly in adult spine clinic because nobody looks for a pediatric diagnosis in a 45-year-old with back pain. Pitfall: transitional anatomy — count from the sacrum and state your convention.

*Field Guide §2.8–2.11*

---

### Module 7 — Scoring the Clinical Half: mJOA

Imaging alone cannot place a patient in a guideline category. Every AO Spine recommendation keys to mJOA, so scoring it is the step that makes the imaging actionable. Two minutes per patient.

- Four domains: UE motor (0–5), LE motor (0–7), UE sensory (0–3), sphincter (0–3) = 18.
- Bands: **mild 15–17, moderate 12–14, severe ≤ 11** — established by ROC analysis across 757 patients at 26 sites. [13]
- Score what the patient *does*. The buttoning and stairs items are concrete for a reason — use them verbatim.
- **A 2-point drop is meaningful** and should prompt a call. Serial mJOA converts "he seems about the same" into a defensible trajectory.

*Field Guide Part 3*

---

### Module 8 — Finding → Guideline → Decision

**The two current sources**, and what each contributes:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Fehlings et al., *Global Spine J* 2017** [36] | Foundational AO Spine / CSRS / AANS-CNS guideline. Four recommendation buckets by severity. |
| **Fehlings et al., *Global Spine J* 2025** [33] | AO Spine Clinical Practice **Recommendations** update. Shifts mild DCM; settles the approach question. |

**The 2017 buckets, verbatim with evidence strength:**

| Category | Recommendation | Strength / Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Moderate & severe (mJOA ≤ 14) | *"We recommend surgical intervention."* | **Strong** / Moderate |
| Mild (15–17) | *"We suggest offering surgical intervention or a supervised trial of structured rehabilitation…"* — with **recommend** operating on deterioration, **suggest** operating on failure to improve | Weak / Very low–low |
| Nonmyelopathic + compression, **no** radiculopathy | *"We suggest **not** offering prophylactic surgery…counseled as to risks of progression, educated about signs and symptoms of myelopathy, and followed clinically."* | Weak / Expert opinion |
| Nonmyelopathic + compression, **with** radiculopathy | *"We suggest offering either surgical intervention or nonoperative treatment consisting of close serial follow-up or a supervised trial of structured rehabilitation."* | Weak / Low |

**What 2025 changes** [33]:
- **Mild DCM:** *"Surgery should be offered as an option"* — conditional, **moderate** quality. Structured follow-up remains viable in the stable patient. The direction of travel is toward earlier intervention; "mild" is no longer a reason to defer the surgical conversation.
- **Approach:** ventral and dorsal are *equivalent* in clinical equipoise — **strong recommendation, high quality (RCT)**. Choose by adverse-event profile in shared decision-making. Underlying trial: **CSM-S** (Ghogawala, *JAMA* 2021) — 163 patients, no SF-36 PCS difference at 1 year (5.90 vs. 6.22, *p* = 0.84); laminoplasty had fewest complications in as-treated analysis. [39]
- **MRI signal change** is endorsed specifically for **counseling on expected recovery**.
- **Multidimensional assessment:** weigh pain, frailty, and HRQoL impact.

**The three rules that sit above every table:**
1. Progressive deficit → surgery, regardless of imaging severity. [6]
2. Imaging severity never overrides a stable exam in the mild/nonmyelopathic bands — no prophylactic surgery for incidental compression. [5,36]
3. Longer symptom duration → worse outcomes. Delay is a decision. [36]

**Delivered as a 4 × 5 decision grid** (imaging finding × mJOA band → recommendation) plus an urgency triage table. Both reproduced in the Field Guide for use in clinic.

*Field Guide Part 5*

### Module 9 — Calibration Cases

Audience calls the finding, the grade, the mJOA band, and the guideline bucket — in that order — before each answer is revealed.

- **Case 1 — Mild myelopathy, significant compression, no signal change.** Tests the 2017-vs-2025 shift on mild DCM.
- **Case 2 — Nonmyelopathic compression with radiculopathy.** Tests whether the room recognizes that radiculopathy moves the patient into a different bucket entirely (~3× progression risk).
- **Case 3 — Moderate myelopathy with multilevel T2 and T1 hypointensity.** Tests counseling language: recovery ceiling, not operative eligibility.

## 5. Take-Home Materials

Participants leave with:

1. **The Field Guide** — full search protocols, recognition atlas with mimics, grading systems, mJOA, and the guideline decision tables.
2. **The structured read template** — a one-page form to complete *before* opening the report, ending in a written commitment: symptomatic level, guideline category, recommendation, confidence, and what was deferred.
3. **The deliberate practice loop** — read cold → commit in writing → open the report → log the delta → review monthly for patterns. With honest calibration expectations: studies 1–10 are humbling, 10–25 build automaticity, 25+ produce useful calibration on core findings.
4. **Practice image libraries** — Radiology Education Asia modules on nerve root compression, denervation, and conus level; Radiopaedia for volume; and the highest-yield library of all, your own department's post-operative studies, where the answer is already known.

## 6. Self-Assessment

1. On sagittal T2 you see a thin bright line running down the center of the cord across five segments. What is your next click, and why? *(Module 4)*
2. A patient has cord compression at C5–6, mJOA 16, stable for a year, no radiculopathy. What did the 2017 guideline say, and what does the 2025 update change? *(Module 8)*
3. Same patient, now with a C6 radiculopathy. What changes, and by roughly how much? *(Modules 6, 8)*
4. Which cord finding most changes what you tell a patient about *expected recovery*, and what is the actual sentence you say? *(Module 4)*
5. Four axial lumbar images: grade each with Schizas. Which grades predict failure of conservative care? *(Module 5)*
6. A multilevel degenerative lumbar MRI with no clear culprit level. Which sequence might localize it, and what are you comparing? *(Module 6b)*
7. Name three findings you should never call yourself, regardless of how confident you are. *(Module 1)*
