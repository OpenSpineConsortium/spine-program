Ready to drop in — cervical cord compression

These four cover most of Part 1 and all three Timeline Panel 2 slots.

The five signal-change types, in one strip Permission

Arvin et al., Spine 2013;38:1170–1176, Figure 1 · arvin2013_p04

A no signal change · B diffuse T2 · C focal T2 · D segmentation of T2 · E low T1 signal. Each panel is a sagittal cervical MRI with the finding arrowed.

Fills: the focal vs diffuse T2 card and the T1 hypointensity card in Part 1. Panels B and C side by side are the focal/diffuse teaching pair; panel E is your T1 image.

Pre- and post-operative pairs Permission

Kato et al., Spine 2018;43:824–831, Figure 2 · kato2018_p04

A→B a 39-year-old after C2–T1 laminectomy and fusion: T2 hyperintensity has resolved; mJOA went 13→16. C→D a 40-year-old after C6/7 ACDF: T1 hypointensity has newly appeared; mJOA 11→13.

Fills: Timeline Panel 2 — the 17.0-month "signal resolved" slot (A→B) and the 39.6-month "T1 irreversible" slot (C→D). This is the exact comparison that figure is built around.

Cord impingement and the banana-shaped cord Permission

Bednarik et al., Eur Spine J 2008;17:421–431, Figure 2 · bednarik2008_p03

Top: axial T2 at C4–C5, ventral impingement from a C4 osteophyte — a concave defect, cord otherwise round. Bottom: ventral compression with a banana-like shape from disc and osteophyte; compression ratio 0.37, cross-sectional area 71 mm².

Fills: the cord deformation card in Part 1 — this is a genuine normal-ish vs flattened pair with the numbers already measured and printed on the image.

Multilevel cervical compression CC BY 4.0

Kwok & Cheung, BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2020;21:823, Figure 1 · kwok2020_p03

Sagittal T2 showing compression at C5–C6 and C6–C7, with the post-operative AP and lateral films after C5–C7 corpectomy and fusion. Freely reusable — BMC publishes under CC BY 4.0.

Fills: a general "this is what multilevel compression looks like" image, and the soft vs hard discussion. Crop the left panel to use the MRI alone.

Ready for later parts

Canonical grading figures. Parts 2 and 3 are largely already solved.

The Schizas grading figure Permission

Schizas et al., Spine 2010;35:1919–1924, Figure 1 · schizas2010_p02

All seven grades — A1, A2, A3, A4, B, C, D — each as a schematic diagram paired with a real axial T2. This is the canonical reference image for the classification.

Fills: the entire Schizas teaching block in Part 2, plus the reference grid for the live grading drill.

Pfirrmann nerve root grading — four figures Permission

Pfirrmann et al., Radiology 2004;230:583–588, Figures 1–4 · pfirrmann2004_p02 ×2, pfirrmann2004_p03 ×2

One figure per grade — 0 no contact (fat layer preserved), 1 contact, 2 deviation, 3 compression — each a labelled diagram beside the matching axial T2.

Fills: the whole nerve-root block in Part 3. Shown above is grade 0; the other three are in the same folder.

The K-line CC BY 4.0

Kwok & Cheung, BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2020, Figure 4 · kwok2020_p08

Lateral radiograph with the K-line drawn from the mid-point of the canal at C2 to C7 in an OPLL patient. Freely reusable. Their Figure 3 (kwok2020_p06_1183x968) shows the canal-occupying ratio measured on axial CT, and Figure 2 (kwok2020_p06_1420x625) shows multilevel OPLL with myelomalacia plus a laminoplasty.

Fills: the OPLL and K-line material wherever you place it. Three free images from one paper — the most useful single source you have.

How the measurements are actually taken Permission

Karpova et al., Spine 2013;38:245–252, Figure 2 · karpova2013_p04

Top: compression ratio on axial T2, both diameters drawn. Bottom: transverse area traced around the cord. Figure 1 (karpova2013_p03) shows MSCC and MCC on the midsagittal.

Fills: the quantitative-metrics section — useful if you want to show that these are drawn by hand, which is part of why reliability varies.

MRI versus CT myelogram, same patient Permission

Crawford et al., JAAOS 2026;34(4):e555–e560, Figures 1–2 · crawford2026_p02, crawford2026_p03

A 55-year-old with congenital stenosis. MRI: 6.7 mm canal, cord:canal > 0.80 — surgical by both thresholds. CTM: 8.3 mm canal, 5.6 mm cord, ratio < 0.80 — not surgical by either.

Fills: the MRI-overestimates-stenosis point. Already split into panels in figures/ with a ready-made comparison pair.

Still missing — your PACS or Radiopaedia

Four slots have no published source. Three of them are the mimic pairs, which is unfortunate because they are the highest-value teaching content in Part 1.

The three mimic pairs No source

Truncation artifact (sagittal showing the central line + axial showing a normal cord) · CSF pulsation artifact (sagittal with flow voids + axial with patent CSF) · Oblique slice (falsely flattened axial + the localiser explaining it).

Nobody publishes these as teaching pairs — they appear as incidental warnings in text. You will have to find them yourself. The good news: artifacts are common, so a scroll through recent cervical studies will turn up truncation and pulsation quickly. Search Radiopaedia for "truncation artifact spine" and "CSF flow artifact" as a fallback.

The four-panel sequence orientation image No source

One cervical level shown as T1, T2, STIR and GRE side by side. No paper publishes this because it is teaching scaffolding rather than a result. Easiest possible PACS pull — any cervical study with all four sequences, one level, four exports. Ten minutes.

Muhle grades 0–3 and cord atrophy No source

Bednarik's Figure 2 gives you two of the four Muhle grades. For a complete 0/1/2/3 set, and for the atrophic-cord-with-roomy-canal image, you need your own cases — but these are easy to find in any spine practice's archive.

The practical read. Your PACS session just shrank from eight Priority-1 assets to roughly two: the mimic pairs and the four-sequence strip. Everything else exists, and four of the images are free to use today with nothing more than a citation.

On permissions. Wolters Kluwer (Spine), RSNA (Radiology), ARRS (AJR), AAOS (JAAOS) and Springer all run through RightsLink. Single figures for a non-commercial CME talk are routinely granted, often free, but each is a separate request. If you want to avoid that entirely, the Kwok images plus your own PACS cases will carry the talk — the published figures are a convenience, not a necessity.
