Scope and Priorities

What to teach, what to mention, and what to leave out

A temporal map of degenerative spine grading systems for the MANS 2026 APP session

Why this document exists

There are, by conservative count, eleven grading systems in play across the source material for this talk: mJOA, Nurick, Muhle, Kang, Schizas, Lee, Wildermuth, Pfirrmann, Torg–Pavlov, compression ratio, and the MCC/MSCC pair. Teaching all eleven to advanced practice providers in fifty minutes would produce a room full of people who remember none of them.

Most of these systems are not competitors. They are sediment — layers deposited by successive eras of thinking about the same problem, each solving a limitation of the one before. Once you see the sequence, it becomes obvious which two or three actually earn a place in an APP’s working memory and which exist mainly so you can recognize the words in a radiology report.

This document lays out that sequence, then converts it into a strict three-tier priority: Learn It, Recognize It, Skip It.

PART 1 — THREE ERAS

Era 1 (1972–2004): How disabled is the patient?

The founding question was clinical, not radiologic — MRI barely existed for most of it.

Nurick (1972) graded patients by walking ability and employability. Six grades, no imaging, entirely about gait. It was the first attempt to make “myelopathy” into a number.

mJOA (Benzel 1991) added upper extremity motor, sensation, and bladder to the lower-extremity focus, producing an 18-point scale across four domains. It measured more of the disease than Nurick did.

Why this era still matters: every treatment recommendation written since 2017 is keyed to the mJOA. The clinical scale is not a historical artifact — it is the input variable.

Era 2 (2004–2013): Can we describe the imaging reliably?

MRI became universal, and the field discovered its embarrassment: radiologists described the same scan differently, and none of the descriptions predicted anything.

The response was a burst of morphologic grading systems, each built on the same insight — stop measuring the container, start describing what is happening to the neural tissue.

  • Pfirrmann (2004) — lumbar nerve root compromise on axial T2: normal → contact → deviation → compression. Validated against surgical findings (r = 0.86).
  • Schizas (2010) — lumbar central stenosis on axial T2 by rootlet/CSF morphology (A1–A4, B, C, D). Explicitly designed to replace area measurement.
  • Lee (2010) — lumbar foraminal stenosis on sagittal T1 by perineural fat obliteration plus root deformity (0–3).
  • Karpova (2013) — tested whether the quantitative measures (TA, CR, MCC, MSCC) were even reproducible.

The defining finding of this era, and the one worth saying out loud: Karpova found that transverse area was the most reliable measure of all (ICC 0.92 intraobserver, 0.86 interobserver) — and it did not correlate with the mJOA at all (r = −0.07, P = 0.80). The number you can measure most reproducibly is the number that tells you least about the patient.

That single result is why Era 3 exists.

Era 3 (2013–present): What actually predicts what happens to this patient?

The field gave up on morphology alone and moved to prognostic signal and guideline-anchored decisions, powered by the AOSpine CSM-North America and CSM-International prospective cohorts (757 patients, 26 sites).

The signal-change story arrived in three installments:

  • Arvin (2013, n = 57) — first prospective blinded look. Low T1 signal, focal T2, and “segmentation” of T2 all predicted worse baseline function. But — counterintuitively — those same patients made the largest gains in gait and balance after surgery.
  • Nouri (2017, n = 419) — the definitive version. Stepwise severity from no signal change → T2-only → T2+T1. T2 hyperintensity alone adds almost nothing. T1 hypointensity predicts reduced recovery (p = 0.03) and lower odds of an optimal outcome (p = 0.005). Multilevel T2 matters; single-level T2 does not.
  • Kato (2018, n = 167) — the postoperative sequel. Resolution of T2 predicts the best recovery (RR 72% vs. 47%); resolution of T1 predicts nothing (38% vs. 26%). And symptom duration tracked directly with irreversibility: 17.0 months (no signal change) → 24.7 (T2) → 39.6 (T1), P = 0.003.

Meanwhile the decision framework consolidated:

  • Fehlings 2017 AO Spine/CSRS guideline — four recommendation buckets by mJOA severity. The reference standard.
  • CSM-S / Ghogawala 2021 (JAMA) — the first RCT of ventral vs. dorsal. No difference.
  • Fehlings 2025 AO Spine recommendations — mild DCM shifts toward surgery; approach equipoise confirmed.
  • Crawford 2026 — MRI overestimates stenosis vs. CT myelography in ~12% of levels.

PART 3 — THE THREE TIERS

🟢 TIER 1 — LEARN IT (four things)

These are the only systems an APP should be able to apply from memory. Everything in the talk should be built to make these four stick.

1. mJOA + the severity bands

Why it wins: it is the input variable for every guideline recommendation written since 2017. Without it, imaging findings cannot be converted into a management category. - Four domains, 18 points. Mild 15–17 · moderate 12–14 · severe ≤ 11. - Teach the MCID properly: it is severity-dependent — 1 point in mild, 2 in moderate, 3 in severe. A flat “2 points” undercalls deterioration in exactly the patients being watched. - Teach the caveat: inter-rater reliability is limited, with disagreement between mild and moderate in over 10% of patients (Martin 2021) — and that is precisely the boundary between “surgery is recommended” and “surgery is offered.”

2. Schizas grading

Why it wins: highest-yield imaging skill that transfers. It is shape recognition, not measurement — no calipers, no software. It predicts something real (failure of conservative care, OR 29.8 for grades C/D or DSCA < 75 mm²). - The whole system reduces to one question: can you still count individual rootlets? Yes → A or B. No → C or D. - Teach the honest reliability: interobserver κ was 0.44 (moderate) in the original paper — 0.67 among readers at the originating unit, with a demonstrated learning effect between readings. This is an argument for teaching it live and practicing it, not for claiming it is easy.

3. The T1/T2 distinction

Why it wins: it is the single finding that changes what you say to a patient, and APPs are usually the ones saying it. - T2 hyperintensity alone ≈ adds little. Multilevel T2 and T1 hypointensity ≈ reduced recovery potential. - The sentence to teach: “This doesn’t mean don’t operate. It means the goal shifts from restoring what you’ve lost toward protecting what you still have.” - Teach the caveat: T1 hypointensity has only fair inter-rater agreement (Fleiss κ = 0.31) and appears in under 20% of patients. It is the most prognostically important finding and one of the hardest to agree on. Say so.

4. The 2017 buckets + what 2025 changed

Why it wins: it is the actual decision. - Moderate/severe (mJOA ≤ 14) → recommend surgery (strong). - Mild (15–17) → suggest surgery or structured rehab (weak) — and per 2025, surgery “should be offered as an option” (conditional, moderate quality). - Nonmyelopathic + compression, no radiculopathy → not prophylactic surgery; counsel and follow. - Nonmyelopathic + compression, with radiculopathy → different bucket, offer either. - Teach “recommend” vs. “suggest” as vocabulary. That distinction is the whole guideline.

🟡 TIER 2 — RECOGNIZE IT (say the name, don’t drill it)

These appear in radiology reports and surgeon conversations. APPs need to know what the words mean and roughly what direction is bad. They do not need to apply them.

⚠️ The Nurick trap — worth 20 seconds of airtime. At least two numbering conventions circulate. The original Nurick (1972) runs 0–5 with grade 0 = root symptoms only. The Tetreault 2017 paper tabulates it as 0–VI, shifting every grade by one. A “Nurick 4” means different things in the two systems. If you see a Nurick grade without a source, confirm the convention.

🔴 TIER 3 — SKIP IT

Leave these out entirely. Mentioning them costs attention and buys nothing.

  • Wildermuth foraminal grading — superseded by Lee, which adds root morphology.
  • Kunogi–Hasue classification — descriptive types without severity grades.
  • Matsuura ratio, laminar roof pitch angle — failed to predict myelopathy.
  • Compression ratio arithmetic — teach the concept (cord shape matters, flattened is worse) and drop the formula. Reliability is decent but it misrepresents deformation when both diameters shrink equally and fails in asymmetric compression.
  • MCC/MSCC formulas — name-recognition only; nobody is computing these in clinic.
  • Signal change ratios (SCR 1.46 / 1.689 cutoffs) — research territory.
  • Hamanishi calculated surface area — historical.

PART 4 — WHAT CHANGED RECENTLY, AND WHAT IT MEANS

If a member of your audience last read about DCM five years ago, these are the four things that are now different.

1. Mild DCM moved toward surgery

2017 offered a choice with very-low-quality evidence. 2025 says surgery “should be offered as an option” on moderate-quality evidence, backed by Badhiwala’s 193-patient mild-DCM cohort and a cost-utility analysis showing +1.26 QALY over a lifetime versus observation.

But teach both sides. In that same cohort the 30-day complication rate was 30% — progression of myelopathy 6.7%, worsening neck pain 6.2%, dysphagia 5.7%. And baseline quality of life in “mild” DCM was impaired to a degree comparable to asthma and congestive heart failure. “Mild” describes the mJOA, not the patient’s life.

2. The approach question is settled — and it’s a shared decision

CSM-S: no difference in SF-36 PCS at 1 year. What differs is the complication profile: ventral 48% vs. dorsal 24% overall, with dysphagia in 41% of ventral patients and half still affected at 6 months. Dorsal laminoplasty looked best in a non-randomized secondary analysis.

For APPs this is a counseling script, not a surgical decision: the approaches work equally well; they fail differently.

3. Advanced imaging moved from “future” to “conditional recommendation”

The 2025 document gives a conditional recommendation to use diffusion-weighted MR signal changes to evaluate prognosis. DTI/DWI is no longer purely aspirational. Soften any “not ready for clinical use” language — say “entering practice, not yet routine.”

4. mJOA severity is no longer the whole picture

Machine-learning phenotyping (Pedro 2024, n = 1047) found four distinct DCM phenotypes — severe multimodal, minimal, motor dominant, pain dominant — and showed that neck pain and frailty, neither captured by the mJOA, drive quality-of-life outcomes. The “severe multimodal” group improved most on mJOA and had the worst quality of life at one year.

The 2025 conditional recommendation follows: consider pain, frailty, and HRQoL when counseling. This is a natural APP role.

Tier 2 gets exactly one slide — a single table of names with one-line meanings, presented as “you will see these words; here’s what they mean; you don’t need to apply them.” Naming the systems you are deliberately not teaching is itself reassuring. It tells the audience the omission was a decision, not an oversight.

PART 6 — THE FRAMING TO OPEN AND CLOSE WITH

Open by naming the sediment problem. Something close to:

“There are eleven grading systems in the literature behind this talk. I’m going to teach you four things and name the rest. The four are the ones that change what you do on Monday.”

That single sentence prevents the most common failure of a talk like this, which is an audience quietly deciding halfway through that the material is not learnable.

Close on the through-line of all three eras:

Era 1 asked how disabled the patient is. Era 2 asked whether we can describe the image reliably. Era 3 asked what actually predicts recovery.

The answer that survived all three: the image tells you what is possible, the patient tells you what is necessary, and the guideline tells you what is recommended. You need all three, and the one most often skipped is the second.
