From Image to Incision: Neuroradiology Guidance for Surgical Planning in Degenerative Cord Compression — Slide-by-Slide Outline with References
Slide 1 — Title Slide
"From Image to Incision: Neuroradiology Guidance for Surgical Planning in Degenerative Cord Compression"

Speaker name, credentials, institution, date
Slide 2 — Learning Objectives
Identify key MRI findings that indicate clinically significant cord compression

Apply imaging grading systems to stratify severity

Integrate imaging with clinical assessment to guide surgical timing and approach

Recognize imaging pitfalls and the limits of MRI-clinical correlation

Slide 3 — The Clinical Problem
Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is the most common cause of spinal cord dysfunction in adults 
[1]

Natural history: 20–62% of patients with symptomatic CSM deteriorate over 3–6 years without surgery 
[2-3]

Asymptomatic cord compression: ~8% develop myelopathy at 1 year, ~23% at 4 years 
[3-4]

Key question: When does imaging warrant surgery?

References: Karadimas et al., Spine 2013; Fehlings et al., Spine 2013; Wilson et al., Spine 2013; Mechas et al., JAAOS 2025 
[1-4]

Slide 4 — Imaging Modalities Overview
MRI: Gold standard — soft tissue detail, cord signal changes, disc/ligament pathology 
[5-6]

CT: Osseous detail, OPLL characterization, surgical planning for instrumentation 
[5]

CT myelography: Alternative when MRI contraindicated; 83–95% agreement with MRI for quantitative stenosis measurements 
[7]

Dynamic (flexion-extension) MRI: Emerging role in detecting positional compression

References: Kane et al., Am Fam Physician 2020; Theodore, NEJM 2020; Crawford et al., JAAOS 2025 
[5-7]

Slide 5 — MRI Sequences and What They Show
T1-weighted: Anatomy, cord atrophy, hypointensity (myelomalacia) 
[8]

T2-weighted: CSF contrast, hyperintense signal change in cord (edema/gliosis) 
[8-9]

T2 (gradient echo): Hemorrhage, calcification

STIR: Ligamentous injury, bone marrow edema

Axial images: Cross-sectional cord morphology, lateral recess/foraminal stenosis; axial T2 images show highest reliability for quantitative measurements 
[10]

References: Nouri et al., Spine 2017; Zhang et al., Spine 2026; Karpova et al., Spine 2013 
[8-10]

Slide 6 — Key MRI Findings in Degenerative Cord Compression
Disc herniation/osteophyte complex effacing the thecal sac

Loss of CSF signal around the cord (circumferential vs. focal)

Cord deformation/flattening

Intramedullary T2 signal change (hyperintensity)

T1 hypointensity (suggests myelomalacia — worse prognosis) 
[8][11]

Cord atrophy (reduced AP diameter)

Use representative images for each finding

References: Nouri et al., Spine 2017; Arvin et al., Spine 2013 
[8][11]

Slide 7 — T2 Signal Change: What Does It Mean?
T2 hyperintensity correlates with neuronal apoptosis and NLRP3-mediated neuroinflammation in animal models 
[9]

T1 hypointensity = myelomalacia/cavitation (generally irreversible); associated with reduced recovery ratio (p = 0.03) and lower likelihood of optimal surgical outcome (p = 0.005) 
[8]

Multilevel T2 change associated with worse baseline severity and recovery potential 
[8]

Postoperative resolution of T2 hyperintensity predicts best outcomes (RR 72% vs. 47%); T1 hypointensity resolution does NOT improve outcomes (RR 38% vs. 26%) 
[12]

Focal T2 signal associated with poorer walking outcomes than diffuse T2 
[11]

Teaching point: T2 signal change alone does not predict outcomes — clinical correlation essential 
[8]

References: Zhang et al., Spine 2026; Nouri et al., Spine 2017; Kato et al., Spine 2018; Arvin et al., Spine 2013 
[8-9][11-12]

Slide 8 — Grading Systems: Modified JOA Score
Clinical severity scale (0–18); classifies mild (15–17), moderate (12–14), severe (0–11) 
[13]

Established by ROC analysis using Nurick score as anchor in 757 patients across 26 sites 
[13]

Drives treatment algorithm: mild/stable → observe; moderate-severe → surgery 
[1][3]

Not an imaging scale but essential for correlating imaging findings with functional status

References: Tetreault et al., Eur Spine J 2017; Fehlings et al., Spine 2013; Mechas et al., JAAOS 2025 
[1][3][13]

Slide 9 — Grading Systems: Imaging-Based Classification
Nurick Grade: 6-grade system (0–5) based on walking difficulty; good correlation with lower limb mJOA (Spearman ρ = 0.90) 
[14-15]

Muhle Classification: Grade 0 (no compression) → Grade 3 (cord compression with signal change)

Kang Grading (cord signal): Grade 0 (no signal change) → Grade 2 (T2 hyper + T1 hypo) 
[8]

MCC and MSCC ratios: Quantitative measures with good-to-moderately high reliability (ICC 0.75–0.88) 
[10]

References: Scheuren et al., Neurology 2021; Revanappa & Rajshekhar, Eur Spine J 2011; Nouri et al., Spine 2017; Karpova et al., Spine 2013 
[8][10][14-15]

Slide 10 — Quantitative MRI Metrics
AP canal diameter: <7 mm suggests surgically relevant stenosis 
[7]

AP cord:canal ratio: >0.8 threshold for surgical consideration 
[7]

Compression ratio (AP/transverse diameter): Lower values = worse deformation; reliable (ICC 0.80) 
[10][16]

MCC and MSCC: Higher values = more severe compromise; MSCC correlates with mJOA, walking time (p < 0.05) 
[10]

Transverse area (TA): Highest reliability of all measures (ICC 0.86) 
[10]

Teaching point: Thresholds are guides, not absolutes — always integrate with clinical picture

References: Crawford et al., JAAOS 2025; Karpova et al., Spine 2013; Liu et al., Neurology 2017 
[7][10][16]

Slide 11 — The Imaging-Clinical Disconnect
52% of asymptomatic adults have disc bulges; 27% have protrusions on lumbar MRI 
[17]

Disc degeneration prevalence: 37% at age 20 → 96% at age 80 
[18]

Cord compression on MRI is the primary driver of surgical decisions despite poor correlation with disease severity 
[19]

Quantitative measures (MCC, MSCC, CR) and descriptive terminology do NOT correlate with mJOA scores 
[19]

Key takeaway: "Treat the patient, not the MRI" — but don't ignore the MRI

References: Jensen et al., NEJM 1994; Brinjikji et al., AJNR 2015; Tempest-Mitchell et al., PLoS One 2019 
[17-19]

Slide 12 — When Imaging Drives Surgery: Indications
Moderate-to-severe myelopathy (mJOA <15) with confirmatory imaging → surgery recommended 
[1][3][6]

Progressive neurologic deficit regardless of imaging severity 
[6]

Newer evidence supports early surgical treatment even in patients with minimal symptoms if imaging confirms cord compression with objective myelopathic signs 
[1]

Imaging findings supporting earlier intervention:

Multilevel compression 
[8]

Combined T2 hyperintensity + T1 hypointensity 
[8][11]

Cord atrophy

Nonsurgical management for moderate-severe myelopathy leads to inferior outcomes vs. surgery 
[3][6]

References: Fehlings et al., Spine 2013; Mechas et al., JAAOS 2025; Theodore, NEJM 2020; Nouri et al., Spine 2017; Arvin et al., Spine 2013 
[1][3][6][8][11]

Slide 13 — When to Watch: The Asymptomatic or Mildly Symptomatic Patient
Incidental cord compression without myelopathy → prophylactic surgery NOT recommended 
[5]

Mild, stable myelopathy → conservative management reasonable with close follow-up 
[5]

Risk factors for progression: coexisting radiculopathy (strongest predictor), electrophysiological abnormalities, large canal stenosis 
[4][20]

Absence of T2 hyperintensity predicts early (<12 mo) myelopathy development; presence of T2 signal predicts later (>12 mo) progression 
[4][20]

Recommended follow-up: serial clinical exams ± repeat MRI at 6–12 month intervals

References: Kane et al., Am Fam Physician 2020; Wilson et al., Spine 2013; Bednarik et al., Eur Spine J 2008 
[4-5][20]

Slide 14 — Imaging for Surgical Approach Selection
Anterior approach (ACDF, corpectomy): 1–2 level anterior compression, kyphotic alignment, pre-existing cervical kyphosis 
[21-22]

Posterior approach (laminectomy ± fusion, laminoplasty): Multilevel compression (≥3 levels), preserved lordosis 
[21][23]

Combined approach: Severe multilevel disease, circumferential compression

Anterior vs. posterior: comparable long-term outcomes (mJOA 15.1 vs. 15.3, p = 0.53); anterior associated with shorter LOS and better lordosis restoration; posterior associated with more C5 palsy 
[22-24]

CT for osseous anatomy and instrumentation planning

Sagittal alignment assessment: C2–C7 SVA, cervical lordosis, T1 slope

References: Kwok & Cheung, BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2020; Kato et al., JBJS 2017; Hitchon et al., Spine 2019; Kouli et al., Spine J 2025 
[21-24]

Slide 15 — Special Considerations
OPLL: CT essential for characterization; posterior approach preferred for K-line (+) with canal-occupying ratio <50–60%; anterior for K-line (−) or canal-occupying ratio >60% 
[21]

Dynamic compression: Flexion-extension MRI may reveal compression not seen on neutral positioning

Adjacent segment disease: Post-surgical surveillance imaging

Thoracic cord compression: Less common but often presents later; disc calcification common

References: Kwok & Cheung, BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2020 
[21]

Slide 16 — Lumbar Stenosis: Imaging Considerations
Schizas classification: 7-grade system (A–D) based on rootlet/CSF ratio on axial T2; grades C and D more likely to fail conservative treatment; grades A and B less likely to warrant surgery 
[25]

Dural sac cross-sectional area: <100 mm² = moderate, <75 mm² = severe; but surface measurements alone can over- or under-diagnose stenosis 
[25]

Schizas and Lee systems: Both show excellent inter-observer agreement and are learnable by clinicians and radiologists 
[26]

MRI findings have no clinically relevant association with patient-reported disability and pain at baseline in the NORDSTEN study 
[27]

Foraminal stenosis: Lee grading (0–3)

References: Schizas et al., Spine 2010; Ko et al., PLoS One 2020; Aaen et al., Eur Spine J 2022 
[25-27]

Slide 17 — Advanced and Emerging Imaging
DTI: FA significantly reduced and ADC increased at compressed levels in CSM vs. controls (meta-analysis of 14 studies, 479 patients) 
[28]

DBSI (Diffusion Basis Spectrum Imaging): 37% of DBSI-clinical correlations significant vs. only 1/36 for DTI; may better reflect baseline disease burden and predict long-term prognosis 
[29]

DTT grading: Correlates with both JOA scores (r = −0.813) and recovery rates (r = −0.429), outperforming conventional MRI grading 
[30]

DTI allows evaluation of microstructural changes not detected on conventional MRI 
[31]

Machine learning–assisted image analysis: Automated stenosis grading (emerging)

References: Guan et al., PLoS One 2014; Zhang et al., Spine 2022; Wang et al., Spine 2017; Shabani et al., J Neurosurg Spine 2020 
[28-31]

Slide 20 — Key Takeaways
MRI is the cornerstone but must be interpreted in clinical context 
[6][19]

T2 signal change and cord atrophy are important prognostic markers, but T2 alone does not predict outcomes 
[8]

T1 hypointensity indicates more permanent injury and decreased recovery potential 
[8][12]

Imaging-clinical mismatch is common — avoid operating on images alone 
[17-19]

Grading systems (mJOA, Nurick, Schizas) provide a shared language between radiologists and surgeons 
[13][15][25]

Earlier surgical intervention for moderate-severe myelopathy yields better outcomes 
[1][3][6]

DTI/DBSI represent promising future biomarkers 
[28-29][31]
