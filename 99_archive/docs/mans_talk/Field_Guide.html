Reading the Scan Before the Read

A Field Guide for Advanced Practice Providers

Recognizing degenerative spine findings on MRI, and converting them into guideline-anchored decisions

Companion handout — MANS 2026 Summer Meeting, APP Session Michigan Association of Neurosurgeons · August 14–16, 2026 · Crystal Mountain Resort

How to Use This Guide

This is a skills manual, not a review article. It exists to close a specific gap: the interval between the moment you open a patient’s MRI and the moment a formal interpretation exists. In that interval, you are frequently the only clinician looking at the images, and decisions get made — who gets worked in today, who gets a surgeon’s phone number, who gets reassured.

The guide has three working parts:

  • Part 1–2 — How to look. A repeatable search protocol and a finding-by-finding recognition atlas: where to look, what it looks like, what fools you.
  • Part 3–5 — What it means. Grading systems, the mJOA, and decision tables tied to the current AO Spine guidelines.
  • Part 6–8 — How to get good at it. A structured reporting template, a deliberate-practice loop, and free image libraries to practice on.

A note on scope. Nothing here makes you a radiologist or authorizes you to render a diagnostic interpretation of record. The formal read is the formal read. What this guide builds is clinical triage literacy — the ability to look at a study and know whether you are dealing with an emergency, a surgical referral, a surveillance patient, or a reassurance conversation, and to have that conversation intelligently with the radiologist and surgeon. Part 1 draws an explicit line between findings you can act on and findings you must defer.

PART 1 — THE SYSTEMATIC READ

1.1 Before you look at anything

Three habits separate a useful read from a misleading one.

Do not read the report first. If you are trying to build recognition skill, reading the impression first destroys the training value of the study — you will see what you were told to see. Read cold, commit to your findings in writing, then open the report. Part 7 formalizes this.

Know the clinical question before you open the study. “Weak hands, dropping objects, wide-based gait, 8 months” makes you look at the cord. “Left leg pain past the knee, worse standing” makes you look at foramina. A read without a question is a tour, not an examination.

Check the study is adequate for the question. No axial T2 through the level of interest? No fat-saturated sequence when you are hunting denervation? A cervical study that stops at C7 in a patient with a thoracic sensory level? Recognizing an inadequate study is itself a clinical skill, and it is one of the highest-value things an APP can catch early, because it saves a wasted clinic visit.

1.2 Sequence orientation — what each one is for

Sequence Fluid Fat What you use it for
T1 dark bright Anatomy, marrow, fatty infiltration, myelomalacia (dark cord), epidural fat
T2 bright bright CSF contrast, cord signal change, disc morphology, rootlet visualization
STIR / T2 fat-sat bright dark Edema — marrow, ligament, denervated muscle. The “something is acutely wrong” sequence
GRE / T2* bright — Hemorrhage, calcification, OPLL (blooms dark)
T1 post-contrast — bright Tumor, infection, post-op scar vs. recurrent disc, acute denervation enhancement

The one-line heuristic: if CSF is bright, you are on T2 or STIR. If fat is also bright, it is T2. If fat is dark, it is STIR or fat-sat.

1.3 Cervical protocol — an eight-step read

Run it in this order, every time. Order matters because it front-loads the findings that change urgency.

Step 1 — Sagittal T2, mid-line. Scroll the whole cord, top to bottom. Ask one question: is there any level where the bright CSF stripe in front of or behind the cord disappears? This single pass finds nearly all significant compression. Mark every level where CSF is effaced.

Step 2 — Sagittal T2. Look at the cord itself at those levels. Any bright signal inside the cord? Note its level and whether it is focal (one segment, crisp) or diffuse (spanning multiple segments, hazy).

Step 3 — Sagittal T1, same levels. Is the cord dark anywhere the T2 was bright? This is the finding with the most prognostic weight and the one most often skipped. It requires deliberately switching sequences at the same level — it will not present itself.

Step 4 — Axial T2 at every level flagged in Step 1. This is where you actually grade compression. Sagittal images overestimate and underestimate; axial is where the cord’s true shape lives. Assess: is CSF gone circumferentially or only anteriorly? Is the cord round, flattened, or crescentic/“banana”-shaped?

Step 5 — Alignment, back on the sagittal. Lordotic, straight, or kyphotic? This is an approach-determining finding, and it takes five seconds.

Step 6 — The compressing element: soft or hard? Disc material follows disc signal and is confined to the disc level. Osteophyte and OPLL are dark on all sequences and extend behind vertebral bodies. If you cannot tell, that is a CT question — and saying so is the correct answer.

Step 7 — Foramina, on the axial and parasagittal. Nerve root compromise at each level. See §2.8.

Step 8 — Everything you were not looking for. Marrow signal, paraspinal soft tissues, the visible portion of the posterior fossa, the lung apices on the lower slices. You are not expected to be definitive here — you are expected to notice that something looks unlike degeneration and escalate it.

1.4 Lumbar protocol — a seven-step read

Step 1 — Sagittal T2, mid-line. Find the conus. Count levels from the sacrum up. Conus tip below the mid-body of L2 is abnormal at any age and reframes everything (§2.11).

Step 2 — Sagittal T2. Scroll for thecal sac caliber. Where does the CSF column narrow or disappear?

Step 3 — Axial T2 at each narrowed level. Grade with Schizas (§2.7). This is the highest-yield single skill in lumbar imaging for an APP, and it is explicitly validated as learnable by non-radiologists.

Step 4 — Parasagittal T2 through both foramina. Grade with Lee (§2.8). Foraminal disease is the classic sagittal-only-if-you-look-for-it miss.

Step 5 — Axial, subarticular/lateral recess zones. The traversing root at each level — displaced, deviated, compressed? (Pfirrmann, §2.8.)

Step 6 — STIR or T2 fat-sat: paraspinal muscle, side to side. Denervation edema localizes the symptomatic level when the anatomy is ambiguous (§2.9).

Step 7 — Marrow, endplates, alignment. Modic change, spondylolisthesis, pars defects, anything non-degenerative.

1.5 The scope line — call vs. defer

Findings an appropriately trained APP can reliably identify and act on: - Presence, level, and number of levels of cord compression - Loss of CSF signal around the cord - Cord deformation and gross atrophy - Presence of T2 hyperintensity and T1 hypointensity in the cord - Schizas grade of lumbar central stenosis - Foraminal narrowing and nerve root contact/deviation/compression - Conus level - Gross alignment: lordosis, kyphosis, listhesis - Study adequacy for the clinical question

Findings you must defer, always: - Any mass, marrow replacement, or enhancing lesion — do not talk yourself into “degenerative” - Infection: discitis/osteomyelitis, epidural abscess, paraspinal collection - Intramedullary lesions that are not clearly compression-related (demyelination, cord tumor, syrinx, infarct) - Anything outside the spine on the visible slices — vascular, renal, pulmonary, retroperitoneal - Post-operative studies where scar vs. recurrent disc is the question

The safety rule: your read exists to raise urgency, never to lower it. If your read is reassuring and the patient is not, the patient wins. A normal-looking MRI does not close a workup — DCM has a recognized rate of normal-appearing initial imaging, and the differential (ALS, MS, B12 deficiency, spinal cord tumor) is not excluded by a degenerative-looking scan.

PART 2 — RECOGNITION ATLAS

Each entry follows the same structure: Where to look → What you see → How to grade → What fools you → Why it matters.

2.1 CSF effacement (loss of the subarachnoid space)

Where to look: Sagittal T2 first for detection; axial T2 to characterize.

What you see: The bright CSF stripe that normally outlines the cord thins, then disappears. Trace the anterior and posterior columns separately — they are effaced independently and the pattern matters.

How to grade — Muhle classification:

Grade Finding
0 Normal subarachnoid space
1 Partial obliteration of the anterior or posterior subarachnoid space
2 Complete obliteration of the subarachnoid space, no cord deformity
3 Cord deformity / impingement

Cord signal change is not part of Muhle — it is graded separately (§2.4).

What fools you: CSF pulsation artifact on sagittal T2 creates dark flow voids that mimic effacement — check the axial before believing it. Volume averaging at the disc level exaggerates narrowing on thick-slice sagittals.

Why it matters: Effacement without deformity is the entry point to the “asymptomatic compression” conversation. It is not, by itself, an operative finding.

2.2 Cord deformation and the compression ratio

Where to look: Axial T2 at the maximally narrowed level. Never grade cord shape off a sagittal.

What you see: The normal cervical cord is a rounded oval, wider side-to-side than front-to-back. Under compression it flattens (“pancake”), then becomes crescentic or “banana”-shaped as the anterior surface is indented.

How to grade — compression ratio (CR):

CR = (smallest AP cord diameter ÷ largest transverse cord diameter) × 100

Lower = worse. Reliability is good (ICC 0.80). [10,16]

What fools you: Obliquity. If the axial slice is not perpendicular to the cord at that level — common at the apex of a kyphosis — the cord looks falsely flattened. Check the slice angle on the sagittal localizer.

Why it matters: Cord shape is more informative than canal diameter, because it reflects what is actually happening to neural tissue rather than to the container.

2.3 Quantitative canal and cord metrics

Reliability ≠ validity — the most important lesson in this section. Karpova tested all four quantitative measures with 7 observers reading 17 studies on 4 occasions. Transverse area was the most reliable measure of all (intraobserver ICC 0.92, interobserver 0.86) — and it showed no correlation whatever with the mJOA (r = −0.07, P = 0.80). The authors concluded their data “do not support TA as a valid measurement instrument.” [10]

Meanwhile MSCC, which was less reliable, correlated best with clinical severity: mJOA r = −0.65 (P = 0.005), walking time r = −0.62 (P = 0.01), number of steps r = −0.55 (P = 0.02). MSCC (cord) beat MCC (canal) — measuring the cord beats measuring the container.

The number you can measure most reproducibly is the number that tells you least about the patient. Reproducibility and meaning are different properties, and this table has both columns for a reason.

Reliability below is given as intraobserver / interobserver ICC. [10]

Metric How it is derived Threshold / meaning Reliability
AP canal diameter Direct measurement, axial or mid-sagittal < 7 mm = surgically relevant stenosis [7] MRI–CTM agreement 87% sagittal, 95% axial [7]
AP cord:canal ratio AP cord diameter ÷ AP canal diameter, same level > 0.8 = surgically relevant stenosis (cord fills > 80% of the canal) [7] MRI–CTM agreement 83% sagittal, 86% axial [7]
Transverse area (TA) Cord cross-sectional area, axial T2 Smaller = worse — but does not correlate with mJOA 0.92 / 0.86 — most reliable

Do not confuse two ratios that share the number 0.8. - Cord:canal occupancy ratio — > 0.8 = stenotic. Cord AP ÷ canal AP. The cord is filling more than 80% of the canal. This is the Crawford surgical threshold. [7] - Torg–Pavlov ratio — < 0.8 = narrow. Canal AP ÷ vertebral body AP. Describes a congenitally small canal, not acquired compression.

Same number, opposite direction, different numerator. Confusing them inverts your conclusion.

MRI vs. CT myelography — and the one clinical rule that comes out of it

Crawford et al. measured 203 levels (C3–C7) in 50 myelopathic patients who had both an MRI and a CT myelogram within 6 months preoperatively. [7]

Threshold Plane MRI–CTM agreement Levels called surgical: MRI Levels called surgical: CTM
< 7 mm canal Sagittal 87% 15% (30/203) 5% (10/203)
< 7 mm canal Axial 95% 6% (13/203) 3% (6/203)
> 0.8 cord:canal Sagittal 83% 17% (35/203) 3% (5/203)
> 0.8 cord:canal Axial 86% 16% (33/203) 3% (5/203)

Three things to take from this table:

1. MRI overestimates stenosis in roughly 12% of levels. That is the paper’s headline conclusion. The direction is consistent and one-sided: on axial images at the 0.8 threshold, MRI flagged 28 levels that CTM did not, while CTM flagged zero levels that MRI missed.

2. Axial beats sagittal. Axial MRI achieved the highest agreement with CTM (95% at the 7 mm threshold). This is independent confirmation of the rule in §1.3 — sagittal finds it, axial grades it.

3. High agreement and a 5-fold difference in positive calls are both true. Most levels are negative on both modalities, which inflates raw agreement while the positives diverge. Do not let “83–95% agreement” reassure you out of noticing that MRI called 35 levels surgical where CTM called 5.

The clinical rule, straight from the paper’s conclusion: “CTM may be useful to verify the degree of stenosis where MRI appears worse than the clinical examination would suggest.” [7]

This is the imaging–clinical disconnect (§2.12) turned into an actionable next step. When the scan looks worse than the patient, that mismatch is not necessarily your error in reading it — the MRI may genuinely be overcalling, and a CT myelogram is the tiebreaker. Otherwise, surgeons can confidently choose either modality based on patient factors.

Two caveats that belong with these numbers. The 7 mm and 0.8 thresholds were developed by group consensus at a single institution, and the authors state plainly that surgical thresholds “are not well defined in the literature.” The study also correlated nothing against clinical status — it does not assess symptoms or neurological examination at all. Treat these as reasonable working numbers for communication, not validated cutoffs for decision-making.

Why these metrics matter — and the honest caveat: They are reproducible, which is not the same as clinically decisive. MCC, MSCC, and CR do not correlate with mJOA scores. [19] Use them to track change over time and to communicate precisely, not to decide who gets an operation.

2.4 Intramedullary T2 hyperintensity

Where to look: Sagittal T2 to detect; axial T2 to confirm it is real and characterize its cross-sectional distribution.

What you see: Bright signal within the cord substance, usually at or just above the level of maximal compression, often centered on the central grey matter.

How to grade — Kang cord signal grade:

Grade Finding
0 No signal change
1 T2 hyperintensity present
2 T2 hyperintensity + T1 hypointensity

Also characterize: focal (single segment, well-defined) vs. diffuse/multilevel (spanning segments, hazy margins), and count the number of levels involved.

What fools you: Truncation (Gibbs) artifact produces a thin bright line down the center of the cord on sagittal T2 that mimics signal change — it is linear, spans many segments uniformly, and disappears on the axial. This is the single most common false-positive cord finding. Always confirm on axial.

This is not a fringe concern: Crawford et al. flag both artifacts explicitly as sources of diagnostic error — CSF pulsation artifact interfering with the diagnosis of cord compression, and truncation artifact making CSF or bone appear thickened and misrepresenting cord atrophy. [7] The defense against both is the same: confirm on a second plane.

Why it matters: - Pathologically, T2 hyperintensity tracks with neuronal apoptosis and NLRP3-mediated neuroinflammation in animal models. [9] - Multilevel T2 change → worse baseline severity (P < 0.0001) and lower recovery ratio (p = 0.001). Present in 27% of patients. [8] - Post-operative resolution of T2 signal predicts the best outcomes (recovery ratio 72% vs. 47%, p = 0.04) — but resolution happens in only 11% of patients. [12] - Single-level T2 change is only weakly predictive. Patients with T2-only change had no significant difference in optimal surgical outcome versus patients with no signal change at all (p = 0.275). [8]

Focal vs. diffuse — get this one right, it is counterintuitive. Arvin found that focal T2 signal predicted worse baseline gait, balance, and mJOA than diffuse or absent signal. But those same patients made the largest post-operative gains in walking time, cadence, and Berg Balance Score. [11] Recovery ratio did not differ significantly between focal, diffuse, and no T2 signal.

So focal T2 is a marker of how bad things are now, not of how much they can improve. Saying “focal T2 means poorer walking outcomes” is wrong. Saying “focal T2 means worse walking now, with real room to improve” is right — and it is a far better thing to tell a patient.

A separate feature, “segmentation” (two discontinuous focal hyperintensities, ~7% of patients), correlates specifically with lower-limb signs and symptoms. [8,11]

2.5 T1 hypointensity (myelomalacia)

Where to look: Sagittal T1, deliberately, at every level where T2 was bright. It will not announce itself.

What you see: Cord signal darker than normal cord at the same level — reflecting cavitation and established myelomalacia rather than reversible edema.

What fools you: Under-windowed T1 images make normal cord look dark. Compare against cord at an uninvolved level in the same image, not against your memory.

Why it matters — this is the highest-value finding in the atlas: - Represents largely irreversible injury (cavitation). - Associated with reduced recovery ratio (p = 0.03) and lower likelihood of an optimal surgical outcome (OR 0.45, p = 0.005, adjusted for baseline mJOA). [8] - Post-operative resolution of T1 hypointensity does not improve outcomes (RR 38% vs. 26%, p = 0.36) — unlike T2. [12] - The 2025 AO Spine recommendations endorse using T1 hypointensity and multilevel T2 change to counsel patients on expected recovery. [33]

The caveat that must travel with this finding. T1 hypointensity is simultaneously the most prognostically important signal change and one of the hardest to agree on. In the Nouri cohort, inter-rater agreement was only fair (Fleiss κ = 0.31) — versus moderate-to-substantial (κ = 0.60) for T2 hyperintensity — and it was present in fewer than 20% of patients, dropping below 10% in mild DCM. [8,12] The authors say plainly that this “limits its utility.”

What that means for you: when you think you see T1 hypointensity, say “possible T1 hypointensity — worth a second look” rather than asserting it. You are calling a finding that experienced raters disagree about a third of the time.

Clinical translation: T1 hypointensity does not mean “do not operate.” It means the conversation changes from “surgery to restore function” toward “surgery to prevent further loss.” Patients need that distinction before they consent, and APPs are frequently the ones who deliver it.

2.6 Cord atrophy

Where to look: Sagittal T2 for global impression; axial T2 with comparison to uninvolved levels.

What you see: Reduced cord caliber, often with a disproportionately large residual CSF space — the canal looks roomy because the cord has shrunk, not because the compression resolved. This is a classic trap: a decompressed-looking canal in a severely myelopathic patient.

What fools you: Normal cord tapering below the cervical enlargement (roughly below C6) is physiologic. Compare to the expected caliber at that level, not to the enlargement above.

Why it matters: Signals chronic, established disease and lower recovery potential; supports earlier intervention when present with other features. [8,11]

2.7 Lumbar central stenosis — Schizas grading

Where to look: Axial T2 at each disc level. This is a shape-recognition task, not a measurement task, which is why it transfers well.

How to grade — Schizas classification (7 grades, A–D):

Grade Rootlets and CSF on axial T2 Interpretation
A1 Rootlets dorsal, occupying < half the sac; CSF clearly visible No or minor stenosis
A2 Rootlets dorsal, in contact with dura, horseshoe configuration No or minor
A3 Rootlets dorsal, occupying > half the sac No or minor
A4 Rootlets central, occupying the majority of the sac No or minor
B Rootlets fill the whole sac but remain individually distinguishable; some CSF gives a grainy appearance Moderate
C No rootlets recognizable — homogeneous grey sac, no CSF signal; posterior epidural fat present Severe
D As C, plus no posterior epidural fat Extreme

The practical shortcut: Can you still count individual rootlets? Yes → A or B. No → C or D.

Supporting measurement: dural sac cross-sectional area < 100 mm² = moderate, < 75 mm² = severe — but area alone both over- and under-calls stenosis, which is precisely why the morphologic system exists. [25]

What fools you: Grading at the pedicle level rather than the disc level (falsely normal). Epidural lipomatosis mimicking grade D. Redundant nerve roots creating a crowded appearance above the actual stenosis.

Why it matters: Grades C and D are more likely to fail conservative treatment — grades C/D or DSCA < 75 mm² carried an odds ratio of 29.8 for failing conservative care and needing surgery. Grades A and B were less likely to warrant surgery. Other predictors of failure: spondylolisthesis (OR 3.3), DSCA at pedicle level < 160 mm² (OR 3.4). [25]

Reliability — the honest numbers. The original paper found interobserver κ 0.44 (moderate) and intraobserver κ 0.65 (substantial). Among readers at the originating unit those rose to 0.67 and 0.77, with a documented learning effect between readings. [25] Observers who received spoken instruction outperformed those given only the written description.

Two things follow. First, do not oversell this as easy — it is learnable, but it is learned. Second, that learning effect is the argument for practicing it live rather than reading a page about it, which is exactly what the drill in the session is for. Later work reports better agreement for both Schizas and Lee and confirms they are learnable by clinicians as well as radiologists. [26]

And the counterweight — from Schizas’ own data, not just NORDSTEN: no correlation was found between stenosis grade (or DSCA) and baseline Oswestry Disability Index, or between grade and surgical outcome. [25] NORDSTEN independently found MRI findings had no clinically relevant association with baseline disability or pain. [27]

Schizas predicts conservative treatment failure. It does not measure how much the patient hurts. Those are different questions and the grade only answers one.

2.8 Nerve root compromise — foraminal and subarticular

Two different systems for two different roots — do not mix them up.

Pfirrmann (2004) Lee (2010)
Root Intraspinal extradural — the traversing root (lateral recess/central) The exiting root in the foramen
Plane Axial T2 Sagittal (parasagittal through the foramen)
Primary sequence T2 T1 — with T2 as a check for false positives from perineural cysts or root swelling
What it grades Disc–root spatial relationship Perineural fat obliteration + root deformity
Reliability Intraobserver κ 0.72–0.77, interobserver κ 0.62–0.67 (substantial); r = 0.86 vs. surgical findings Interobserver κ 0.91–1.0, intraobserver κ 0.80–1.0 (nearly perfect)

Where to look: Parasagittal T1 through each foramen for Lee grading (perineural fat is bright on T1 — that is why T1 is the primary sequence), plus axial T2 through the disc and subarticular zone for Pfirrmann. Foraminal disease is missed when only mid-sagittal and axial-at-disc images are reviewed.

The three-step relationship. Describe what the disc is doing to the nerve, not just what the disc is doing: [32]

  1. No contact — disc extends into the foramen, nerve untouched
  2. Contact — disc touches the root without deforming it
  3. Compression — disc deforms or compresses the root

Formalized — Pfirrmann nerve root compromise grading: [34]

Grade Finding
0 Normal — no contact between disc material and root
1 Contact — disc contacts the root, root in normal position
2 Deviation — root displaced dorsally by disc material
3 Compression — root compressed between disc and canal wall; may be flattened or indistinct

Formalized — Lee foraminal stenosis grading: [35]

Grade Finding
0 Normal
1 Mild — perineural fat obliteration in two opposing directions, no root morphologic change
2 Moderate — perineural fat obliteration in four directions, no root morphologic change
3 Severe — root collapse or morphologic change

The key concept for both systems: perineural fat is your contrast agent. On T1, the normal root sits in a bright fat halo within the foramen. Grading is essentially asking how much of that halo is gone, and is the root itself deformed?

What fools you: Foraminal veins mimicking retained fat. Obliquely oriented foramina at L5–S1 requiring angled review. Failing to distinguish the exiting root (foraminal, e.g. L4 at L4–5) from the traversing root (subarticular, e.g. L5 at L4–5) — this determines which level you operate on, and getting it backward is a wrong-level error.

Why it matters — the number that justifies this whole section:

Boos et al. compared symptomatic disc herniation patients against age-, sex-, and occupation-matched asymptomatic controls. Disc herniation was present in 76% of the asymptomatic group. The only substantial morphologic difference between symptomatic and asymptomatic subjects was the presence of neural compromise — 83% vs. 22%. [34]

That is the entire argument in one comparison. The disc is not the finding. The disc–nerve relationship is the finding.

It sharpens further by grade: contact alone (Pfirrmann grade 1) is seen in 22–23% of asymptomatic volunteers, while frank root compression is rare in asymptomatic people. [34] So grade 1 is close to noise; grades 2–3 carry the signal. Pfirrmann’s own reliability data agrees — the most frequent disagreement was between normal and contact, and the least frequent was between deviation and compression.

In DCM specifically: coexisting radiculopathy is the strongest predictor of progression in nonmyelopathic patients with cord compression (RR ≈ 3.0) and moves the patient into a different guideline bucket entirely (§5.2). [4,20,36]

Limitation to carry: Lee’s grading was validated for reproducibility without symptomatic correlation — the authors state this explicitly. It tells you the foramen is narrow, not that it is the pain generator. [35]

2.9 Paraspinal denervation edema — the level localizer

Where to look: STIR or T2 fat-sat, axial, comparing left to right at each level. Post-contrast T1 if available.

What you see, by phase: [37]

Phase T2 fat-sat / STIR T1 Contrast
Acute–subacute (~first 2 weeks) Hyperintense (edema pattern) Iso-intense Enhances
Chronic Edema resolves Fatty infiltration, volume loss —

Volume loss and fatty infiltration begin early — even within the acute window.

Technique: side-to-side comparison at the same level is essential. The finding is a difference, and it is nearly invisible if you assess one side in isolation.

What fools you: Inadequate fat suppression producing artifactual bright muscle — check whether suppression is uniform across the image. Recent injections, EMG needling, or surgery produce identical edema. Bilateral disease removes your internal control.

Why it matters: Denervation localizes the symptomatic root when a multilevel degenerative study will not tell you which level is the pain generator. It is the imaging analogue of EMG paraspinal mapping.

The stated pitfall: readers focused on canal pathology routinely overlook denervation changes, and without dedicated fat-saturated sequences or contrast they are easily missed — particularly in the acute phase. [37] Which is exactly why an APP who knows to look for it adds real value: you can request the sequence.

2.10 OPLL and the K-line

Where to look: Sagittal T1 and T2 for the dark retrovertebral band; CT is essential for definitive characterization. GRE shows blooming.

What you see: An ossified band posterior to the vertebral bodies, dark on all MRI sequences, extending behind vertebral bodies rather than being confined to disc levels — this is the distinction from disc-osteophyte complex.

The K-line: a line connecting the midpoints of the spinal canal at C2 and C7 on the sagittal image. - K-line (+) — OPLL does not cross the line. Posterior decompression can work: the cord has room to drift backward. - K-line (−) — OPLL crosses the line. Posterior decompression alone will fail, because there is nowhere for the cord to drift.

The canal-occupying ratio: maximal ossification thickness ÷ AP spinal canal diameter, measured on axial CT. [21]

Why it matters — this is directly approach-determining: [21]

Finding Preferred approach
K-line (−) — regardless of occupying ratio Anterior
K-line (+) and occupying ratio > 60% Anterior
K-line (+) and occupying ratio < 50–60% Posterior

The number that makes this concrete: among OPLL patients treated by posterior decompression, mean recovery rate was 13.9% in K-line (−) versus 66% in K-line (+) (P < 0.01). Separately, canal occupancy > 60% yielded a 14% recovery rate after laminoplasty versus 58% below 60% (P < 0.03). A posterior decompression in the wrong OPLL patient does not merely underperform — it largely fails, because the cord has nowhere to drift.

Two counseling numbers from the same review: dural tear complicates anterior OPLL surgery in 4.3–32% of cases versus 0.5–3% posteriorly; C5 palsy after posterior surgery runs 1.4–23%, with risk factors of older age, male sex, OPLL, preoperative foraminal stenosis, pre-existing deltoid weakness, and laminectomy with fusion. [21]

A practical non-OPLL point worth carrying: for degenerative disease generally, the posterior approach is favored in osteoporosis, chronic renal failure, and smokers — anterior instrumentation fails at high rates in poor bone. [21]

Source caveat: Kwok & Cheung is a narrative review whose own conclusion is that the evidence remains inconclusive. No study has yet examined K-line and occupying ratio together.

2.11 Conus medullaris level

Where to look: Sagittal T2, mid-line. Count vertebral levels from the sacrum upward. Five seconds.

The rule: [38] - At birth: typically L1/2, may be L2/3 - By 2 months: ~L1/2 - At any age: at or above the mid-body of L2 is normal - Caudal to mid-L2 → concern for tethering

What fools you: Transitional lumbosacral anatomy makes level-counting unreliable — always count from the sacrum and state your counting convention. Miscounting is the most common source of a false “low conus.”

Why it matters: A low conus reframes a “degenerative” presentation entirely, and it is skipped constantly in adult spine clinic because nobody is looking for a pediatric diagnosis in a 40-year-old with back pain.

2.12 The base rate problem — what “abnormal” is worth

Before you attach significance to any degenerative finding, calibrate against how common it is in people with no symptoms at all:

  • 52% of asymptomatic adults have lumbar disc bulges; 27% have protrusions [17]
  • Disc degeneration prevalence: 37% at age 20 → 96% at age 80 [18]

The interpretive consequence: finding degeneration on an MRI is roughly as informative as finding grey hair. The finding only acquires meaning when it explains this patient’s specific syndrome at the anatomically corresponding level. This is why §1.1 insists you know the clinical question first.

PART 4 — NURICK AND THE SUPPORTING SCALES

Nurick grade (0–5) — ambulation-focused, fast, correlates well with lower-limb mJOA (ρ = 0.90): [14,15]

Grade
0 Root signs/symptoms, no cord involvement
1 Cord signs, no walking difficulty
2 Slight walking difficulty, employment unaffected
3 Walking difficulty preventing employment, not requiring assistance
4 Able to walk only with assistance
5 Chair-bound or bedbound

⚠️ Two numbering conventions are in circulation — confirm which one you are reading. The original Nurick (1972) runs 0–5 as above, with grade 0 = root symptoms only. Tetreault 2017 tabulates the scale as 0–VI, adding a grade 0 for “no root or cord symptoms” and shifting everything else up by one — so their Grade I = original grade 0, their Grade VI = original grade 5. [13]

A “Nurick 4” therefore means walks only with assistance in one convention and gait abnormality prevents employment in the other. If a Nurick grade appears without a stated source, confirm the convention before acting on it. This is exactly the kind of shared-language failure the talk is about.

Use Nurick as a fast trajectory marker between visits; use mJOA when you need the number that maps to a guideline recommendation.

PART 4B — SEP AND MEP: HOW TO OBTAIN THEM, AND THE PUBLISHED WORKUP ALGORITHM

Electrophysiology carries more predictive weight in nonmyelopathic cord compression than any imaging measurement (§5.6). APPs are frequently the ones ordering it, so this section covers what the two studies measure, how to actually get them, and the published algorithm they slot into.

4B.1 What the two studies measure

SEP — somatosensory evoked potential MEP — motor evoked potential
Stimulus Electrical, at a peripheral mixed nerve — median at the wrist, tibial at the ankle Magnetic, via a coil over the motor cortex; then repeated at the nerve root
Recording Surface electrodes at successive points along the pathway: Erb’s point (N9) → cervical cord (N13) → brainstem (P14) → sensory cortex (N20). Lower limb: lumbar cord (N22) → cortex (P40) Surface electrodes over abductor digiti minimi (upper limb) and abductor hallucis (lower limb)
Pathway tested Dorsal columns — ascending sensory Corticospinal tract — descending motor
Key measure Central sensory conduction time: N13→N20 (arm), N22→P40 (leg) Central motor conduction time (CMCT) = cortical latency − root latency
Abnormal means Absent N13, P14 or N20; prolonged N13–N20 interpeak latency; abnormal P9/N13 or right–left N20 amplitude ratio — all with a normal N9 Prolonged CMCT and/or abnormal MEP:CMAP amplitude ratio
Predictive weight [20] RR 3.21 (1.75–5.87) RR 2.91 (1.60–5.29)
Availability Widely available in neurophysiology labs Limited — largely academic centres

The localising logic is identical in both, and it is the thing to check in the report. An abnormal central response with a normal peripheral response places the lesion in the cord rather than the nerve. If the report does not state that the peripheral response (N9 for median SEP, N22 for tibial) was normal, the central finding is uninterpretable — call the lab.

4B.2 How to obtain them

Who performs them. A clinical neurophysiology laboratory — usually staffed by a neurologist or physiatrist with neurophysiology fellowship training. Many academic neurosurgery departments run their own service through their intraoperative monitoring team, which is often the faster route if your institution has one.

What to write on the requisition. The lab will run a standard protocol unless you specify otherwise, and the standard protocol may not report central conduction times. Ask explicitly:

“Cervical cord compression on MRI without clinical myelopathy. Please perform upper and lower limb somatosensory evoked potentials (median and tibial) and motor evoked potentials with transcranial magnetic stimulation, and report central sensory and central motor conduction times. Clinical question: is there subclinical central conduction deficit indicating cord dysfunction?”

Naming the clinical question matters — “rule out myelopathy” gets you a different report than “quantify central conduction.”

Coding. Typical codes are 95925/95926/95938 for upper, lower, and combined SEP, and 95928/95929/95939 for upper, lower, and combined transcranial-stimulation MEP. Verify current codes with your billing office — these change, and I have not confirmed them against a current schedule.

Timing. Unlike needle studies, evoked potentials do not require a waiting period after symptom onset — they measure conduction, not denervation, so they can be obtained immediately.

Safety screen before MEP. Transcranial magnetic stimulation is contraindicated with intracranial ferromagnetic material, cochlear implants, and implanted pacemakers or neurostimulators. A history of epilepsy is a relative contraindication; seizure risk is low but real.

If MEP is unavailable — which it often is outside academic centres — the fallback is genuinely reassuring. Clinically symptomatic radiculopathy alone carries a higher relative risk (3.68) than either evoked-potential study, and requires nothing but an examination. [20] SEP is far more widely available than MEP; obtaining SEP alone still adds independent predictive information.

Entry point B — radiculopathy or axial neck pain, no MRI yet

This is the authors’ own inversion of the usual order, and it is the part most often missed:

“Electrophysiological evaluation of cervical cord dysfunction in patients with cervical radiculopathy or back pain is valuable… these methods are highly recommended, not only in patients with MRI-documented asymptomatic cord compression, but also in patients with radiculopathy or back pain before MRI examination. The presence of ‘central’ myelopathic SEP or MEP abnormalities would justify further MRI examination.” [20]

So: radiculopathy or axial neck pain → SEP/MEP → central abnormality present → obtain cervical MRI. The evoked potentials are being used as a screening test for cord involvement in a population presenting with root or axial symptoms.

For the watch-and-wait patient: - Serial mJOA at every visit — a documented number, not an impression - Risk-stratify the interval rather than applying a blanket one. A stronger statement than the 2017 buckets convey. The Wilson systematic review issued a strong recommendation on moderate evidence: nonmyelopathic patients with cord compression who have “clinical or electrophysiological evidence of cervical radicular dysfunction or central conduction deficits seem to be at higher risk for developing myelopathy and should be counseled to consider surgical treatment.” [4] Surveyed spine surgeons agreed — of 774 respondents, the majority considered symptomatic radiculopathy predictive of progression. And here is the finding that should reframe how you use imaging in this group. In the same cohort, not one quantitative imaging parameter predicted progression — Pavlov ratio (P = 0.49), compression ratio (P = 0.15), cross-sectional cord area (P = 0.96), number of stenotic levels (P = 0.30), or type of compression (P = 0.20). In nonmyelopathic patients with cord compression, the scan does not tell you who will convert. The examination does. That is why the guideline bucket turns on radiculopathy rather than on stenosis severity.

The T2-absence finding — interesting, but do not teach it as actionable. Bednarik’s multivariate model found that MRI hyperintensity decreased the risk of early (< 12 mo) progression, being associated instead with later (> 12 mo) manifestation. [20] Three caveats travel with it: on univariate analysis it was not significant for early progression (OR 0.41, P = 0.25); for the primary endpoint it reached only P = 0.049 against the authors’ own Bonferroni-corrected threshold of α < 0.01; and the Wilson systematic review, having reviewed exactly this evidence, concluded that “in light of this discrepancy, no definite recommendation can be made surrounding the utility of this finding.” [4] Mention it as a curiosity if at all; do not let anyone leave planning care around it.

PART 7 — THE DELIBERATE PRACTICE LOOP

Recognition is a trained skill with a known training method. Reading about findings does not produce it; committing to calls and getting corrected does.

The loop — per study, about 10 minutes:

  1. Open cold. No report, no prior reports, no colleague’s opinion. Clinical question only.
  2. Run the protocol (§1.3 or §1.4). Do not shortcut the order — the order is what makes it reproducible under time pressure.
  3. Commit in writing using the Part 6 template. Include your confidence level. An uncommitted read teaches nothing.
  4. Open the report. Compare line by line.
  5. Log the delta in a running miss log:

Calibration expectations, so you do not quit at study four: - Studies 1–10: humbling. You will miss foraminal disease and T1 hypointensity, and you will over-call cord signal because of truncation artifact. This is the expected curve, not evidence you cannot do it. - Studies 10–25: the protocol becomes automatic; misses shift from “did not look” to “looked and was unsure.” - Studies 25+: useful calibration on the core findings. You will know which findings you are reliable on and which you always defer — which is the actual goal.

Three accelerators: - Practice on studies where you already know the outcome. Post-operative patients are ideal — you know what was found and what was done. - Read alongside the radiologist when you can. Ten minutes in the reading room outperforms an hour of review articles. Most radiologists welcome the interest. - Track over-calls as carefully as misses. An APP who over-calls loses credibility with the surgeon faster than one who occasionally misses, and over-calling drives unnecessary imaging and referrals.

The honest ceiling: this loop makes you reliable at triage-relevant recognition on degenerative studies. It does not make you reliable at excluding tumor, infection, or demyelination, and no amount of repetition on degenerative spines will. Keep §1.5 in view.

PART 9 — POCKET SUMMARY

The eight-step cervical read: CSF effacement → cord T2 → cord T1 → axial at every flagged level → alignment → soft vs. hard → foramina → everything else.

The three findings that change the conversation most: 1. T1 hypointensity — irreversible injury; changes counseling from restoration to prevention 2. Radiculopathy with cord compression — moves a nonmyelopathic patient into a different guideline bucket 3. Any documented deterioration — overrides every imaging consideration

The numbers:

mJOA bands mild 15–17 · moderate 12–14 · severe ≤ 11
mJOA MCID 1 pt mild · 2 pts moderate · 3 pts severe
mJOA mild-vs-moderate disagreement > 10% between raters
Cervical AP canal diameter < 7 mm surgically relevant
Cord:canal occupancy ratio > 0.8 surgically relevant (cord ÷ canal)
Torg–Pavlov ratio < 0.8 congenitally narrow canal (canal ÷ vertebral body)
MRI vs. CTM agreement 83–95% (highest on axial, 95%)
MRI overestimation of stenosis ~12% of levels — CTM verifies when MRI looks worse than the exam
Lumbar dural sac CSA < 100 mm² moderate · < 75 mm² severe
Schizas C/D more likely to fail conservative care
Conus at or above mid-L2, any age
Surveillance MRI 6–12 months
Asymptomatic disc bulge 52%
Untreated symptomatic CSM deterioration 20–62% over 3–6 yrs
Asymptomatic compression → myelopathy ~8% at 1 yr · ~23% at median 44 months
Radiculopathy → myelopathy (RR) 3.68 (OR 4.69 for progression within 12 months)
Radiculopathy → myelopathy risk ~3×

The two sentences to leave with: > Degeneration is nearly universal on imaging and only means something when it explains this patient’s syndrome at that level. > A finding changes management when it changes the mJOA category, the trajectory, or the guideline bucket — otherwise it is description.
