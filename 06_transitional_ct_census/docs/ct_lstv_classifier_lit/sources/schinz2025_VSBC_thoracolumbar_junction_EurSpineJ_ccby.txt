Abstract
Purpose The	correct	identification	of	the	thoracolumbar	junction	is	mandatory	for	labeling	of	the	lumbar	spine,	particularly	
if	visualized	incompletely.	The	current	state-of-the-art	relies	on	a	continuous	counting-based	(CCBC),	a	rib-length-based	
(RLBC),	or	rib-based	classification	(RBC)	of	the	spine	enumeration.	However,	the	CCBC	and	RBC	often	lead	to	inconsistent	
labels	depending	on	the	visualized	spine	segments.
Methods In	this	retrospective	study,	three	classifications	for	assessing	the	thoracolumbar	junction	were	evaluated	in	CT	
scans	from	2005	to	2018,	covering	the	complete	thoracolumbar	spine:	(1)	CCBC,	(2)	RLBC/RBC,	and	(3)	a	vertebra-shape-
based	classification	(VSBC).	The	classifications	were	compared	regarding	consistent	labels	depending	on	the	visualized	
spine	segments,	agreement	with	nerve	morphology,	and	inter-rater	reliability.
Results CT-imaging	data	from	1,242	subjects	(mean	age,	63	years	±	12;	771	women)	were	included.	The	VSBC	led	to	more	
consistent	labels	than	all	other	classifications.	Only	the	VSBC	perfectly	complied	with	the	nerve	morphology	(VSBC,	100%;	
RBC,	97.2%,	RLBC,	96.3%;	CCBC,	92.6%).	The	VSBC	had	slightly	superior	inter-rater	reliability	than	the	RBC	(0.61	vs.	
0.59).
Conclusion The	VSBC	performs	more	reliably	and	consistently	than	other	classifications	in	labeling	the	thoracolumbar	
junction,	even	for	anatomical	variants	or	incompletely	visualized	spines.	Compared	with	rib-based	classifications,	the	VSBC	
demonstrated	modest	but	clear	advantages	in	consistency	and	agreement	with	nerve	morphology.
Key points
 ● Several	classifications	identify	the	thoracolumbar	vertebral	junction,	causing	spine	enumeration	inconsistencies.
 ● Shape-based	classification	uses	several	vertebral	features	to	identify	the	thoracolumbar	junction.
 ● Vertebral	shape-based	classification	perfectly	aligns	with	nerve	morphology.
 ● Vertebral	shape-based	classification	is	more	reliable	than	other	methods.

Introduction
The	spine´s	anatomy	is	highly	variable,	with	numerical	
aberrations	seen	in	about	8%	of	the	population	[1–3].	At	the	
thoracolumbar	junction	(TJ)	–	the	transition	zone	between	
the	thoracic	and	lumbar	spine	(T11-L2)	–	some	vertebrae	
frequently	exhibit	both	thoracic	and	lumbar	characteristics,	
thus	labeled	as	thoracolumbar	transitional	vertebrae	(TLTV)	
[4].
Correct	identification	of	vertebrae	is	an	important	task,	
especially	in	TLTV.	This	ensures	correct	labeling	for	pre-
operative	planning	of	spinal	surgery	or	interventions	and	
prevents	operating	on	unintended	levels	[5,	6].	The	current	
clinical standard for labeling is counting the vertebrae in 
a	cranial-to-caudal	manner	with	the	assumption	of	seven	
cervical	and	twelve	thoracic	vertebrae	[7,	8].	However,	in	
patients	with	anatomical	variations	or	when	the	spine	is	
incompletely	imaged,	this	may	lead	to	discrepancies.	Fur-
thermore,	surgeons	often	identify	vertebral	levels	by	the	
ribs,	which	a	simple	vertebral	counting	does	not	address.	
Some	ribs	may	be	underdeveloped,	i.e.,	stump	ribs,	or	con-
fused	with	additional	ossification	centers	arising	from	the	
transverse	processes	[3].	To	address	this,	a	widely	adopted	
nomenclature	[9]	classifies	the	TJ	based	on	rib	length	and	
morphology.	Vertebrae	with	at	least	one	rib	longer	than	
38	mm	are	considered	true	thoracic.	Those	with	ribs	shorter	
than	38	mm	but	with	a	rib-vertebra-joint	are	labeled	as	
TLTV	and	included	in	the	thoracic	spine.	Alternative	meth-
ods	focus	on	other	features	such	as	rib	shape,	ossification	
centers,	vertebral	morphology,	or	nerve	pathways	[3,	10,	
11].	However,	these	approaches	may	conflict	in	TLTV,	for	
instance,	when	one	side	has	a	rib	and	the	other	an	accessory	
ossification	center	or	lumbar	transverse	process	[4,	12].
Recently,	morphologic	features	were	described	that	help	
differentiate	thoracic	and	lumbar	vertebrae.	These	include	
vertebral	body	shape,	shape	of	transverse	processes,	and	
orientation	of	superior	facet	joint	(SFJ)	[4,	12,	13].	How-
ever,	there	is	no	comparison	of	the	reliability	and	accuracy	
of	the	different	classification	systems,	especially	for	cases	
involving	TLTV.
Therefore,	this	study	aims	to	evaluate	different	classifica-
tion	systems	for	the	TJ	on	CT	imaging,	including	a	newly	
proposed	vertebra-shape-based	classification	(VSBC).	To	
validate	the	results,	we	evaluated	agreement	with	nerve	
morphology	using	MRI	[11].	We	hypothesized	that	the	
VSBC	performs	more	reliably	regardless	of	the	imaged	
spine	segments,	conforms	greater	with	the	nerve	morphol-
ogy,	and	results	in	less	enumeration	shifts.
Methods
The	retrospective	study,	using	public	and	in-house	CT-
datasets,	was	approved	by	the	local	ethics	committee	that	
waived	the	need	for	informed	consent.
We	hypothesized,	that	the	VSBC	performs	more	reliably	
regardless	of	the	imaged	spine	segments,	conforms	greater	
with	the	nerve	morphology,	and	results	in	less	enumeration	
shifts.
Participants
The	public	dataset	(d1)	[12,	14]	and	two	in-house	CT	data-
sets	(d2-d3)	from	2005–2018	were	included	(Fig.	1).	Data-
set d1	has	been	specifically	collected	to	represent	subjects	
with	spine	anatomical	variants,	such	as	stump	ribs,	cervical	
ribs,	TLTV,	and	lumbosacral	transitional	vertebrae.	Datas-
ets d2 and d3 represent a non-preselected cohort of routine 
CT	scans.	Inclusion	criteria	involved	subject	age	older	than	
18	years;	a	minimum	spatial	resolution	of	2.0	mm	(cranio-
caudal	and	anterior–posterior	direction),	and	5.0	mm	(left–
right	direction),	and	a	fully	imaged	thoracic	and	lumbar	
spine.	As	numeric	aberrations	of	the	cervical	spine	are	rare	
a	correct	identification	of	the	first	thoracic	vertebra	can	be	
achieved	by	assessing	the	first	true	rib	even	in	incompletely	
imaged	cervical	spines	[12,	15].

Method	2	(Rib-based	nomenclature):	Divided	into	(2.1)	
rib-based	 classification	 (RBC)	 following	 Wigh	 et	 al.'s	
nomenclature	[9],	and	(2.2)	rib-length-based	classification	
(RLBC).	 RBC	 labels	 a	 vertebra	 with	 true rib-vertebra-
articulation	but	a	rib-length	below	38	mm	as	TLTV	and	
includes	it	in	the	thoracic	spine,	while	RLBC	labels	a	ver-
tebra	with	true rib-vertebra-articulation but both rib lengths 
below	38	mm	as	a	lumbar	vertebra	with	stump	ribs.	As	these	
nomenclatures	often	get	mixed-up	in	clinical	practice,	this	
may	lead	to	discrepant	annotations	between	both	rib-based	
classification	systems.
Method	3	(VSBC):	Assesses	vertebral	morphology	at	the	
TJ	based	on	three	anatomical	key	aspects:	(I)	Shape	and	ori-
entation	of	the	SFJ,	(II)	shape	and	position	of	the	transverse	
and	costal	processes	relative	to	the	SFJ,	and	(III)	presence	
of	a	costal	fovea	(Fig.	2).
I)	 In	the	thoracic	spine,	the	plane	of	the	SFJ	is	approxi-
mately	co-planar	with	the	coronal	plane,	whereas	in	the	
lumbar	spine	the	plane	of	the	SFJ	is	curved	but	more	
co-planar	with	the	sagittal	plane	[12].	In	some	cases,	
the	SFJ	may	exhibit	an	intermediate	shape,	transitioning	
from a thoracic angle to a lumbar angle in cranial-to-
caudal	direction.
II)	 In	thoracic	vertebrae,	the	transverse	process	originates	
at	the	same	level	as	the	SFJ	and	extends	posteriorly,	
forming an overriding facet alignment visible in sagittal 
view.	In	contrast,	lumbar	vertebrae	show	the	SFJ	dorsal	
to	a	laterally	oriented	costal	process,	without	a	visible	
inferior	facet	in	sagittal	view	(free	facet).	In	TLTV,	both	
transverse	and	costal	processes	may	be	present	simulta-
neously,	typically	with	a	smaller	laterally	oriented	cos-
tal	process	and	a	larger	posterior	transverse	process.
Each	thoracic-type	feature	scores	one	point.	After	scoring	
all	three	features	(range	0-3),	rib-vertebra	articulation	is	
evaluated:	a	true	rib	joint	with	rib	length	>38mm	confirms	
thoracic	status	in	vertebrae	scoring	≥2.	Vertebrae	scoring	≤1	
without	a	rib	joint	or	with	ribs	≤38mm	are	classified	as	lum-
bar.	Vertebrae	with	mixed	feature	scores,	ambiguous	SFJ	
shapes,	or	partial	ribs	are	labeled	TLTV	and	further	subdi-
vided	based	on	the	dominant	morphological	score.

Results
Participants
Out	of	the	total	2,076	subjects	from	all	datasets,	1,242	had	
fully	imaged	thoracic	and	lumbar	vertebrae.	Table	2 pres-
ents the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics 
of	the	subjects.
Performance comparison of the methodological 
approaches
The	CCBC	resulted	in	discrepant	labels	in	13.3%	of	cases	
(38.2%	in	 d1,	10.0%	in	 d2,	and	10.3%	in	 d3).	RBC	and	
RLBC	led	to	discrepant	labels	in	4.3%	of	cases	(9.6%	in	d1,	
1.9%	in	d2,	and	4.0%	in	d3;	Table	3).
The	CCBC	led	to	enumeration	shifts	in	1	case	with	26	
free	presacral	vertebrae	(0.1%),	RBC	and	RLBC	in	17	cases	
(1.4%)	and	40	cases	(3.2%),	respectively,	and	VSBC	in	18	
cases	(1.4%;	Table	4).
Comparison with nerve morphology on MRI
Out	of	the	110	subjects	with	MRI,	L5	nerve	identification	on	
sagittal	and	axial	slices	was	possible	in	108	cases.	Applying	
nerve	morphology	led	to	the	enumeration	of	one	11	T/5L,	
four	11	T/6L,	94	12	T/5L,	seven	12	T/6L,	and	two	13	T/5L	
cases.	The	agreement	with	the	different	labeling	methods	
was	as	follows:	CCBC,	100/108	(92.6%);	RBC,	105/108	
(97.2%);	RLBC,	104/108	(96.3%);	and	VSBC,	108/108	
(100%).	An	example	for	the	assessment	of	the	nerve	mor-
phology	is	presented	in	Fig.	4.
Inter-rater reliability
As	 the	 inter-rater-reproducibility	 was	 excellent	 for	 all	
scoring	methods	when	considering	the	complete	dataset	
of	experience	(rater	4,	1	and	3)	without	a	previous	train-
ing	session.	For	this	reading	test,	cut-outs	containing	only	
three	vertebrae,	including	ribs	and	surrounding	soft	tissues	
at	the	TJ	were	visible	to	the	readers	without	information

Control analysis using nerve morphology in MRI
In	110	cases	from	dataset	d3,	additional	MRI	of	the	lumbar	
spine	were	available	to	assess	the	nerve	morphology	as	pro-
posed	by	Peckham	et	al.	[11].	In	brief,	the	L4,	L5,	and	S1	
nerves	were	identified	by	their	morphology.	The	L4	nerve	
was	identified	by	its	proximal	split	into	the	peroneal	branch,	
which	joins	the	lateral	aspect	of	the	L5	nerve	and	its	smaller	
caliber	of	<	50%	of	the	caliber	of	the	L5	nerve.	The	L5	nerve	
was	identified	by	its	great	caliber	and	its	non-splitting	proxi-
mal	course.	The	S1	nerve	was	identified	as	the	consecutive	
nerve	caudally	to	the	L5	nerve	and	its	slightly	smaller	cali-
ber	in	comparison	with	the	L5	nerve.

Importantly,	the	VSBC	perfectly	aligned	with	functional	
parameters	assessed	on	MRI,	namely	nerve	morphology.
Accurate	labeling	of	vertebrae	is	critical	for	communi-
cation	between	clinicians	and	for	safe	treatment	planning.	
This	is	particularly	challenging	in	TLTV,	which	combine	
features	of	both	thoracic	and	lumbar	levels	[4].	Our	study	
confirmed	these	challenges,	as	discrepant	annotations	fre-
quently	occurred	depending	on	which	classification	systems	
(CCBC	or	RBC)	was	applied,	with	some	authors	suggesting	
whole	spine	imaging	to	determine	correct	spine	enumera-
tion	[18].	The	VSBC,	in	contrast,	applies	distinct	morpho-
logical	features	–	such	as	SFJ	orientation	or	configuration	
of	transverse	processes	–	which	allows	consistent	labeling.	
Unlike	rib-based	methods,	the	VSBC	is	not	restricted	by	
assumptions	about	rib	length	[9].	Remarkably,	the	VSBC	
achieved	perfect	agreement	with	nerve-morphology-based	
labeling,	reinforcing	its	validity.	Interestingly,	a	previous	
publication	 proposing	 a	 nerve-morphology-based	 classi-
fication	 only	reached	a	near-perfect	agreement	of	92.15%	
(106/108	cases)	[11].	The	two	failed	cases	were	TLTV	with	
supernumerary	ribs	(T13	vertebrae),	which	the	VSBC	suc-
cessfully	accounts	for.
We	observed	moderate	inter-rater	reliability	for	both	the	
RBC	and	VSBC.	Although	Wigh	et	al.	provided	a	definition	
for	TLTV	by	rib	length	measurement,	our	findings	showed	
that	interpretations	among	readers	still	varied,	particularly	
in	cases	with	stump	ribs,	additional	ossification	centers,	or	
intermediate	morphology.	This	likely	reflects	different	inter-
pretations	of	a	"true"	rib	and	varying	levels	of	reader	famil-
iarity	with	TLTV.
The	RBC,	originally	proposed	for	standard	radiographs,	
is	inconsistently	applied	in	CT-imaging,	and	some	authors	
have	already	introduced	shape-based	aspects	accompanying	
the	standard	rib-based	methods	[12].	According	to	the	origi-
nal	RBC,	a	vertebra	with	a	stump	rib	is	counted	as	TLTV,	
included	in	the	thoracic	spine	[9],	which	however,	excludes	
the	possibility	of	a	lumbar	vertebra	with	a	stump	rib	–	an	
issue	resolved	by	the	VSBC.
Therefore,	the	VSBC	offers	practical	advantages.	It	pro-
vides	accurate	and	reliable	annotations	even	in	incompletely	
imaged	spines,	consistent	with	functional	anatomy,	i.e.,	
functional	innervation	of	the	lower	extremity	and	anatomy	
of	the	lumbosacral	plexus.	Because	it	is	independent	of	rib	
(k	>	0.9),	we	focused	our	evaluation	on	the	transitional	cases	
only.	For	the	46	vertebrae	with	a	thoracolumbar	transitional	
anomaly,	the	average	Cohen's	kappa	for	the	RBC	was	0.59	
(range	0.44–0.83)	and	0.61	for	the	VSBC	(range	0.45–0.94,	
Table 5).	All	kappa	scores	were	determined	as	statistically	
significant.

reliability.

approach	for	identifying	thoracic,	lumbar,	and	TLTV.	While	
its	improvements	over	rib-based	classifications	in	TLTV	are
