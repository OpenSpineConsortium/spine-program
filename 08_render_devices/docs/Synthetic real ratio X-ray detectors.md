# Synthetic gains plateau near ten-to-one

The measured optimum for mixing synthetic with scarce real X-ray data sits between **4:1 and 10:1 synthetic-to-real at the image level**, no sweep in any modality has shown a gain past 10:1, and three sweeps found a slight loss beyond it — but **no fluoroscopy or DRR paper has ever swept that ratio**. Every flagship physics-simulation study (DeepDRR, SyntheX, Pelphix, FluoroSAM, Toth, i3PosNet, Kausch, Rapuri) trains on renders alone and tests on real frames, so the window comes from generative chest-radiograph work, one guidewire study, dermatology and street-scene detectors. Real-image returns diminish in the mid-hundreds: a synthetic-pretrained landmark model carries **2.6–4.3× lower error than one trained from scratch at 100–400 real radiographs**, and the advantage is gone by 600–700; 240 real fluoroscopy frames lifted a synthetic-pretrained segmenter from Dice 0.10 to 0.55; about 100 radiographs re-trained a YOLOv5s to mAP above 0.95 on large bones. For the rare class, the only spine recipe with numbers is VERIDAH's **4× epoch-level oversampling** of a few dozen real anomaly cases (96–97% L6-type and T13-type recall, 99.2% of CT scans fully correct, no measured cost to normal anatomy); the two medical dose-response curves peak at 4× and saturate at 10:1, and parity rebalancing collapses majority-class sensitivity to 0.35. What moved L6 from undetectable to detectable in VerSe was **85 distinct scans**, not weighting, and T13 with six scans never stabilised. Physics fidelity halves sim-to-real landmark error over naive renders, but only under strong domain randomisation; style transfer is renderer-independent and adds nothing at scale; no study has varied fidelity and ratio together. Renders per CT flattened between 250 and 500 in the single curve that exists, and distinct-CT count has never been ablated. The recommendation at the end — full-bank synthetic pretraining under strong domain randomisation, full-depth fine-tuning on a mixture holding real frames at 10–20%, 4× oversampling of real transitional frames, rare-class prevalence raised to a quarter to a third of the render bank through distinct CTs rather than duplication, 200–500 renders per CT, and 300–500 labelled real frames per device — rests on adjacent evidence at every step, and the report says where.

## No DRR paper has ever swept the synthetic-to-real ratio

Every paper that defines physics-simulated X-ray training uses one protocol: render from CT, train on renders alone, test on real frames. DeepDRR trained a pelvis landmark network on **20,000 DRRs from 20 CTs** and tested on 78 real cadaver X-rays with no real image in training; the realistic renderer cut mean landmark error from 78.2 ± 26.5 mm to 24.7 ± 21.3 mm (p < 0.01), but the ablation runs over rendering physics, never over a real-image count ([Unberath 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7297499/)). SyntheX varies only the synthetic side — 366 pose-matched renders versus a scaled set of 10,000 to 20,000 images from 20 CTs, the paper stating the count both ways — against a fixed pool of 366 real hip fluoroscopy images from six cadavers used for leave-one-specimen-out testing; it contains no mixed synthetic-plus-real run and no reduced-real curve, and positions synthetic data as "a viable alternative to real data training, or at a minimum, a strong candidate for pre-training" ([SyntheX](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06127)). Pelphix trained on 229,488 simulated frames, reached 93.8% on simulation and 67.6% in cadaver, and named real-sequence fine-tuning as future work ([Pelphix](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09285)); FluoroSAM trained from scratch on 3 M synthetic X-rays ([FluoroSAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08059)); i3PosNet's 10,000 synthetic images gave 0.024 mm position error on synthetic and 0.746 mm on real phantom frames with "no fine-tuning or mixed training experiments" ([i3PosNet](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7316684/)); Toth, Kausch, Zhou and Rapuri follow the same pattern, and one 2025 pelvic-landmark paper whose "fine-tuned" model is a single epoch of a pose loss has no real fluoroscopy anywhere in it ([Mo 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21575v1)). The one spine fluoroscopy study that fine-tunes on real frames, Jecklin's lumbar 3-D reconstruction, uses a single real count — 3,317 paired ex-vivo images from ten specimens on top of 120,148 DRRs from 863 CTs — and reports F1 0.84 after full fine-tuning with no variation of the fraction ([Jecklin 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.16027)). A manuscript sentence of the form "no published DRR-based study sweeps the synthetic-to-real mixing ratio or the real-image count" is supported by full-text reads of all of these.

Two regularities cross the table. When synthetic and real images are trained jointly, the gain flattens at a synthetic share of roughly **4:1 to 10:1** and beyond that is flat or slightly negative. VDSB-GWSyn's endpoint accuracy went from 52.6% with no synthetic pretraining to 86.3% at 2,000 synthetic images on 182 real frames and slipped to 85.3% at 3,000 ([Tang 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00109)). RoentGen's 1.1k real plus 1.1k synthetic (AUROC 0.77) beat 1.1k plus 5.5k (0.76), and 1.1k synthetic alone (0.69) trailed 1.1k real alone (0.73) ([RoentGen](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2211.12737)). Moroianu's out-of-distribution AUROC plateaued at 300k synthetic on 66k real, "about 5×", while in-distribution AUROC never moved ([Moroianu 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12676388/)). When synthetic data serve as pretraining and real data as fine-tuning, the ratio stops mattering: 565k synthetic followed by fewer than 10k real matched the 66k real-only model ([Moroianu 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12676388/)), and 100,000 domain-randomised renders followed by 6,000 real gave AP 98.5 against 96.5 for real-only ([Tremblay 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06516)). The joint-mix window and the pretrain-then-fine-tune freedom are different answers to the same question, and any ratio quoted in the manuscript must say which scheme it belongs to.

Harm from synthetic data appears in three forms, none of them a mixed DRR run gone wrong. Poor simulation is worse than good simulation: naive DRR 78.2 mm against DeepDRR 24.7 mm ([Unberath 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7297499/)), and SyntheX's naive renders under strong randomisation reached 20.55 mm against 8.15 mm for the real-trained model ([SyntheX](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06127)). More synthetic data can over-fit the synthetic domain — Toth attributes worse clinical registration error to "training on significantly more data, causing the network to overfit more to the synthetic domain" ([Toth 2019](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v102/toth19a/toth19a.pdf)). And synthetic-only training trails real training in-distribution while helping out-of-distribution: Moroianu's 565k synthetic-only model scored 0.756 against 0.772 on MIMIC (p < 0.05) but 0.702 against 0.661 on NIH ([Moroianu 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12676388/)), and SyntheX's COVID lesion segmentation reached 85.0% accuracy against 94.0% for real training ([SyntheX](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06127)). No fluoroscopy paper reports a mixed synthetic-plus-real run that was worse than real-only, because none was run.

## Real-image returns flatten between 300 and 700 labelled frames

The only real-count learning curve for an X-ray landmark task with synthetic pretraining is Barr's cervical-spine study, which pretrained a 23-keypoint model on DDPM samples generated from 4,963 real lateral films and then fine-tuned on n real images from an external set, testing on 374 held-out radiographs ([Barr 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13185266/)). The curve is the single most useful number set in this literature for a few-hundred-real-frames setting.

| Real images n | Fine-tuned from synthetic backbone, MSE | De novo, MSE | Error ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (zero-shot) | 124.73 | — | — |
| 100 | 31.78 | 84.33 | 2.7× |
| 200 | 12.97 | 55.84 | 4.3× |
| 300 | 10.10 | 34.30 | 3.4× |
| 400 | 9.74 | 27.21 | 2.8× |
| 500 | 7.49 | 14.17 | 1.9× |
| 600 | 8.56 | 7.42 | 0.9× |
| 700 | 4.37 | 7.42 | 1.7× |

The synthetic backbone won at six of seven sizes and lost only at n = 600; the value of 7.42 appears for de novo at both 600 and 700 in the fetched summary and should be checked against the paper's table before it is quoted ([Barr 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13185266/)). Read as a whole, the curve says that **below about 400 real images a synthetic prior is worth a 2.6–4.3× error reduction, and by 600–700 real images the prior has bought roughly 100–200 real images and nothing more**: the fine-tuned model at 500 real (7.49) sits between de novo at 600 and 700 (both 7.42). This is in-domain generative augmentation, not physics rendering, and the manuscript should say so when it cites the crossover.

The fluoroscopy evidence is a set of single points that land in the same range. Gherardini's catheter segmenter, pretrained on 9,000 composited synthetic frames and fine-tuned on **240 in-vivo frames** drawn across six procedures, reached mean Dice 0.55 (0.58–0.78 on the four well-exposed procedures) where the same architecture trained on the in-vivo frames alone gave about 0.10 ([Gherardini 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7903142/)). SyntheX's real-only baseline, trained leave-one-specimen-out on roughly 300 real images per fold, lost to 10,000 renders with no real data at all in the preprint (6.29 vs 8.15 mm, p = 0.032) ([SyntheX](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06127)), and in the published version with 60 extra Loop-X images the two were at parity (6.44 ± 7.05 vs 6.46 ± 8.21 mm) ([SyntheX, Nature MI](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10959504/)); either way, 300 real frames from five specimens was not enough to beat a large synthetic corpus for this task. On the detector side, a YOLOv5s re-trained by transfer learning on carpal radiographs crossed precision, recall and mAP of 0.95 at **96 to 100 radiographs**, with the small first carpal bone needing twice the epochs ([carpal YOLOv5s](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.08734)); this is a floor for large, always-present, high-contrast bones, not a general saturation point. Tremblay's fine-tuning ladder on domain-randomised pretraining — 100 real images to AP 84.2, 1,000 to 88.9, 6,000 to 98.5 — shows the curve still climbing through the thousands for a harder detection task ([Tremblay 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06516)).

Three results bound the regime from the other side. In dermatology, Sagers found that "the primary driver of performance was the number of real images", that gains "saturated at synthetic-to-real data ratios above 10:1", and that 32 real images per class with 10× synthetic matched 64 real images per class (29.4% vs 26.3%, p = 0.39) — one real image bought roughly what ten synthetic ones did at the low end ([Sagers 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12453)). Hadzic's hand-landmark study with only 60 real images found DDPM augmentation moved point error from 0.82 to 0.84 mm, a null result at the smallest real count ([Hadzic 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12489)). And at the other extreme, adding CT-derived DRRs to 3,434 real chest radiographs at 8% or 37% synthetic share left clean-test bone-segmentation Dice unchanged (92.58 real-only vs 91.96 and 92.30 mixed); the gain appeared only on rotated synthetic views the real set lacked ([CXR bone segmentation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11519576/)). Once real data reach the thousands, synthetic DRRs buy pose coverage, not accuracy on the real distribution.

Nobody has measured what 10, 50 or 100 real frames buy on top of a DeepDRR-class corpus. Every fluoroscopy fine-tuning point uses hundreds to thousands of frames and a single N, and Gherardini's apparent inverse relation between fine-tuning count and Dice (240 frames → 0.55; 579 or 714 frames → 0.17–0.26) is a procedure-shift artefact of which sequences were held out, not a count curve ([Gherardini 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7903142/)). The small-N regime that a per-device product depends on is unpublished for this modality.

## Fourfold oversampling lifts rare-vertebra recall; parity rebalancing costs the common classes

No published spine paper renders, generates or simulates L6, T13, sacralised L5 or Castellvi cases as training data and reports per-class recall before and after; searches combining "synthetic", "simulated anomaly", "augmentation" and "oversampling" with the variant names returned nothing, and no deep-learning paper reports per-Castellvi-type sensitivity at all. The two quantified levers in the spine literature are more distinct real cases and epoch-level oversampling of the ones you have.

The first lever is the VerSe story. In VerSe'19, "None of the methods ... identified the presence of L6"; in VerSe'20, "almost all algorithms identify at least a fraction of the L6 vertebrae", and the organisers attribute the change to data, not method: "Supervised algorithms fail to generalise to out-of-distribution cases (L6 in VerSe'19) when their percentage of occurrence in the dataset is consistent with their low clinical prevalence," while over-representation "makes better algorithm design and learning feasible" ([VerSe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09193)). VerSe'20 carries L6 in **85 scans** (28 training, 38 public, 29 private) against 165 ending at L5, about a third of scans with a visible lumbosacral junction, a prevalence roughly five to ten times clinical; T13 is present in only 6 scans (2 in training) ([Liebl 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8553749/)), and for T13 "the identification rate widely varies between the Public and Hidden phases for all teams" ([VerSe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09193)). The worst-case VerSe'20 scan is an L6 case, and VerSe'19 methods run on VerSe'20 data show how a counting-based method fails whole-scan on it (Lessmann 66.96 identification rate; "In cases with L6, this affects the entire scan") ([VerSe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09193)). The enrichment factor that worked was achieved with distinct real scans, not duplication or synthesis, and it is confounded with a year of method development.

The second lever is VERIDAH, the anomaly-aware extension of SPINEPS, which is the closest published analogue to the oversampling question. Its CT training cohort of 1,171 subjects contained 60 thoracic-enumeration-anomaly (5.1%) and 83 lumbar-enumeration-anomaly (7.1%) cases; "each anomalous case in the training set [was sampled] four times during each epoch"; and T13 and L6 were removed from the label space, "indicated by two consecutive T12 or L5 labels", because training on T13 directly "would create a huge class imbalance problem", with a separate transition-type head carrying the variant. On a deliberately enriched real CT test set (25 TEA, 29 LEA) it reached **96.30% TEA recall, 97.22% LEA recall and 99.18% of scans perfectly labelled**, with perfect labelling on normal cases only "marginally higher" than on anomalies ([VERIDAH](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14066v1)). There is no ablation of the factor; 4× is a design choice that produced no measurable cost, not a measured optimum.

The dose-response curves for synthetic rare-class supplementation come from chest radiography and dermatology and agree on shape. Dushenev adapted a diffusion model on about 50 real positives of one rare ChestX-ray14 pathology and swept the synthetic positive ratio on a ResNet-18 with inverse-ratio class weighting, testing on real images only: recall rose from 0.130 at 0× to **0.744 at 4×**, then fell to 0.570 at 10× and 0.540 at 20×, while PR-AUC dropped from 0.846 to 0.677 at 4× ([Dushenev 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11898)). The falling PR-AUC says much of the recall gain is an operating-point shift toward the rare class rather than better ranking — the manuscript should report rare-class recall alongside PR-AUC and common-class precision, or the number will flatter itself. Sagers' gains were largest with 16–32 real images per class, significant at 10 synthetic per real (+13.3% absolute), and "neither monotonic nor statistically significant" from 25× to 75× ([Sagers 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12453)). Khosravi's 1× to 10× CheXpert sweep was monotone but small in aggregate (0.782 → 0.804 AUROC), with "the largest increase in performance ... in pathologies with < 5% prevalence" ([Khosravi 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11177083/)).

The cost side is measured most cleanly outside medicine and once inside it. LVIS repeat-factor sampling at its default threshold t = 0.001 raised rare-category AP from 3.2 to 13.4 at a frequent-category cost of 0.6 AP; the same scheme at t = 0.1 lowered every group, and class-aware sampling — full rebalancing — bought less rare AP (8.5) and lowered both common and frequent groups ([LVIS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.03195)). Chang's re-run on Mask R-CNN puts the frequent-class price of class-aware sampling at 7.1 mask AP (29.0 → 21.9) against 0.4 for RFS ([Chang 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05702)). The default RFS factor is mild by construction: a category in 0.01% of images is repeated 3.2×, and 10× is reached only at one image in 100,000. On a YOLOv5s, class-aware and repeat-factor sampling "do not outperform the baseline" on COCO-ZIPF, weighted BCE caused "significant performance degradation" until its weight was cut tenfold, and mosaic and mixup improved performance "uniformly across" classes ([COCO-ZIPF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07113)). The medical instance is ISIC 2019: a baseline with "aggressive inverse-frequency oversampling" hit DF sensitivity 0.92 and VASC 1.00 while **nevus sensitivity fell to 0.35**; replacing it with class-balanced focal loss and square-root effective-number sampling gave nevus 0.929, DF 0.880, VASC 0.875 and higher AUC for every class ([ISIC 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13337696/)). Buda's classical result that CNNs should be oversampled "to the level that completely eliminates the imbalance" without overfitting held on MNIST and CIFAR but not on ImageNet, where oversampling scored below baseline at every imbalance ratio tested ([Buda 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1710.05381)).

Whether duplication of too few cases fails where distinct views succeed is the question the manuscript most needs answered, and the evidence is mixed and thin. Duplication does not overfit in the classical sense — VERIDAH's 4× works, Buda saw no train-test gap widening — but it moves the decision boundary, which is the ISIC 2019 price. Distinct views add variability duplication cannot: Frid-Adar's 480 geometric views per liver lesion saturated accuracy at 78.6% by 5,000 samples per fold, "where adding more augmented data examples failed to improve the classification results", and only GAN samples on top moved it to 85.7%, with the gain landing on the confusable classes (metastasis sensitivity 68.7 → 81.2%, haemangioma 72.3 → 78.5%) and specificity rising alongside ([Frid-Adar 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.02385)). On ISIC 2016, plain random oversampling gave malignant recall 0.63 and macro-F1 0.72 against 0.74–0.75 and 0.90 for SMOTE and ADASYN ([ISIC 2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.15837)). The one paper that designed the exact comparison — Sagers' duplication control against 1, 3 or 7 synthetic images per real rare image — put the result in a supplementary figure that was not retrievable ([Sagers 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12453)). Generators fitted to about 50 examples can emit near-copies (unconditional latent diffusion models "memorize patient imaging data") ([Dar 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01054)), so the dichotomy is a continuum, and Dushenev's falling PR-AUC at high ratios is what near-duplicate synthetic positives would produce. The VerSe contrast — L6 identifiable with 85 distinct scans, T13 unstable with 6 — points to the number of distinct cases, not the sampling weight, as the binding constraint, and this project's own unpublished observation of L6 Dice 0.000 under 12× duplication of too few distinct cases sits in the same direction without being citable.

## Physics and style lower the floor but have never moved the ratio

The only tabulated naive-versus-physics comparison on real images is SyntheX's precisely matched benchmark, where synthetic images were rendered at the registered poses of 366 real hip fluoroscopy images so that they "only differ in the realism of the simulation". At 360 px with strong domain randomisation the realistic DeepDRR renderer gave **9.05 ± 19.37 mm against 20.55 ± 35.92 mm for naive DRRs**, and the real-trained model 8.15 ± 15.30 mm; at 180 px the same column reads 8.41 vs 13.03 vs 7.78 mm ([SyntheX](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06127)). The gain is not monotone: with regular randomisation at 180 px naive (13.12) beat realistic (14.33). "Training with strong domain randomization results in much worse performance" only on naive images, because bone contrast "is already much less pronounced in naive simulations" ([SyntheX](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06127)). The physics term, then, is what lets the renders survive strong appearance randomisation; it was not sufficient on its own, and the win over real training came only when the realistic renderer was scaled to 500 renders per CT — 6.29 ± 6.29 mm in the preprint ([SyntheX](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06127)), parity at 6.44 vs 6.46 mm in the published version with 60 additional Loop-X images ([SyntheX, Nature MI](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10959504/)). Toth's ladder makes the same point without any physics: on 21 clinical patients his non-randomised network's mean translational error was 18.45 mm with 342 outliers, intensity randomisation alone brought it to 2.42 mm, and intensity plus collimation randomisation to 0.75 mm with 30 outliers, using perturbations that were "unrealistic ... such that the target domain becomes just another variation" ([Toth 2019](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v102/toth19a/toth19a.pdf)). A 2026 registration-pretraining paper reports the same ladder on DeepFluoro: no randomisation 76.2% sub-millimetre success, plus intensity 79.6%, plus projection-physics randomisation 83.2%, full scheme 84.7% ([arXiv 2607.23343](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.23343)). The DeepDRR ablation that would isolate scatter, the most expensive physics term, sits behind a paywall; an indexed snippet says its improvement was "not statistically significant" ([DeepDRR IJCARS](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11548-019-02011-2)).

Style transfer is nearly renderer-independent and adds nothing at scale. In the matched benchmark, CycleGAN took naive DRRs from 16.22 mm to 7.63 mm and realistic ones from 12.62 mm to 8.57 mm, with the authors noting the two "achieve comparable performance ... images via CycleGAN have similar appearance" whatever the input; but on the scaled corpus CycleGAN reached 6.59 ± 7.25 mm against 6.29 ± 6.29 mm for physics plus strong randomisation, "at the disadvantage that real data with sufficient variability must be available at training time" ([SyntheX](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06127)). Feature-level adaptation (ADDA) was worse than strong randomisation in five of six cells. RadRotator trained a CycleGAN on 250,000 DRRs and 250,000 hip radiographs and abandoned it because "the content of the original image was significantly altered" in some cases, replacing it with a histogram-shift augmentation ([RadRotator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13000)). In the one spine fluoroscopy study that ran both, real-to-synthetic style transfer and full fine-tuning on real frames gave identical F1 0.84 ([Jecklin 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.16027)). Rapuri's DeepFluoro experiment is the cleanest evidence on what synthetic data actually buys: at matched poses the plain DRR arm was worst (7.83 mm at NCC 0.60 against 4.89 mm real-trained), and with a "cloud" of 500 extra views per patient at ±2.5° it became best at the lower thresholds (6.02 and 9.73 mm at NCC 0.40 and 0.20 against 9.50 and 18.94 mm for real training) ([Rapuri 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.21414v1)). Pose variety, which renders have and real frames do not, mattered more than the rendering source.

Whether calibrated fidelity shifts the crossover with real data is unmeasured: no paper varies naive, physics and style-matched rendering against a synthetic-to-real ratio in one design, and no DeepDRR-class learning curve over real fine-tuning images exists for any X-ray detection task. Physics-calibrated noise as augmentation of real radiographs for detection is likewise unreported; the nearest measurement is CT nodule detection, where noise-power-spectrum-matched augmentation beat none on real low-dose scans (CPM 0.677 vs 0.586, ΔCPM 0.09, 95% CI 0.02–0.17) but not white Gaussian noise (0.677 vs 0.633, CI −0.04 to 0.12) ([PMC13366036](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13366036/)) — the same shape as the scatter finding: the calibrated term is not demonstrably better than a cruder one once some noise is present. The practical reading is that fidelity sets the floor of the synthetic-only regime and the tolerance for randomisation, and that the ratio recommendation below is independent of it because nothing measured says otherwise.

## Detectors saturate near 100 easy radiographs, classifiers near 20,000, renders near 500 per CT

No source measures a learning curve for a YOLO-family detector on radiographs at 1024 px. The closest curves are for chest-radiograph classifiers and for one small detector. Two large classification studies place the knee near **20,000 images**: normal/abnormal CXR AUC was 0.84 at 2,000, 0.95 at 20,000 and 0.96 at 200,000, the last "not significantly different" from 20,000 ([Dunnmon 2019](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/full/10.1148/radiol.2018181422)); pneumothorax AUCs "increased rapidly from 2 × 10³ to 20 × 10³ training samples, with more gradual increase" to 291,454, ResNet-50 going 0.86 → 0.95 over that last decade ([J Digit Imaging 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35239091/)). The pretraining source moved the curve far more than N did: CXR-specific SupCon pretraining "reduced label requirements up to 688-fold", reaching AUC 0.95 for tuberculosis from 45 radiographs ([Sellergren 2022](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/full/10.1148/radiol.212482)). For CXR classification, maximum AUCs fell "between 256 × 256 and 448 × 448 pixels" on ResNet34 and DenseNet121 ([Sabottke & Spieler 2020](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/full/10.1148/ryai.2019190015)), so a ResNet-18 per-object classifier on 1024-px frames is not resolution-limited; the resolution cost falls on the detector. Medical segmentation follows E(N) = aN^−b + c with "earlier and task-dependent performance saturation" and "a persistent error floor" ([arXiv 2511.13883](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.13883)), and fitted 3-D scaling exponents range 3.5× across anatomies (β 0.209 to 0.724) ([arXiv 2605.06859](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06859v1)) — an exponent measured on one anatomy does not transfer, so the spine-fluoroscopy curve must be fitted on the project's own subsets.

On the synthetic side, the single render-scaling curve is SyntheX's: with 20 CTs fixed at 500 renders each and subsets of 1,000 / 2,000 / 5,000 / 10,000 images, landmark error went **6.61 → 6.56 → 5.82 → 5.95 mm** while Dice went 0.82 → 0.81 → 0.82 → 0.86 ([SyntheX, Nature MI](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10959504/)). Landmark error flattened between 250 and 500 renders per CT; segmentation was still improving. Because CT count was never varied, the experiment cannot say whether that is a render-diversity ceiling or a 20-patient ceiling. Renders per CT in published pipelines span two orders of magnitude with no comparison across the axis: 10 (Toth, 1,000 CTs), about 830 (Pelphix, 337 CTs), 1,024 (agentic C-arm landmarks, 50 CTs), about 1,850 (FluoroSAM, 1,621 CTs) ([Toth 2019](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v102/toth19a.html); [Pelphix](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11016332/); [arXiv 2604.18740](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18740); [FluoroSAM](https://papers.miccai.org/miccai-2025/0344-Paper5042.html)). Anyone claiming a distinct-source ceiling for DRR training would be the first to measure it.

No X-ray paper measures real-image upweighting, a progressive synthetic-to-real ramp, or any mixing schedule against alternatives; the variable every X-ray sim-to-real paper measured was the strength of domain randomisation. The head-to-head comparisons of fine-tuning against joint training come from general detection. On Cityscapes with a COCO-pretrained YOLOv3, pretrain-then-fine-tune and joint mixing showed "no significant difference" (mAP50 0.371 ± 0.008 vs 0.384 ± 0.013), and "the best ratio of real data in a mixed training dataset is between 5% and 20%" ([arXiv 2202.00632](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00632)). With only 160 real images, two-stage "Bridged Transfer Learning" "generally outperforms the mixed approach ... especially in low-real-data regimes", and realistic synthetic data alone was "far worse than their real-data-only counterparts on in-distribution test data" ([arXiv 2510.12208](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.12208v1)). Freezing early layers during real fine-tuning "actually decreased rather than increased performance" by up to 13.5% ([Tremblay 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06516)), and Jecklin found freezing modules "showed inferior results compared to full fine-tuning" ([Jecklin 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.16027)); Gherardini fine-tuned only the deepest seven layers but at a single N, so depth and data volume were never separated ([Gherardini 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7903142/)). FleXray, the largest DRR-plus-real segmentation corpus, reports that "mixing online and offline rendering performs best" and that generatively enhanced renders added 2.2 to 7.4 Dice on ribs 5–10 while equal-dataset mean Dice was 0.870 with or without them, but it never sweeps the real share ([FleXray](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.26756)). The one radiograph study that mixed rendered (non-DRR) images into a YOLO-pose model added about 30% synthetic and cut YOLO26x-pose error by 17.8% (p = 0.002), with the authors' own caveat that the mixed set had 30% more gradient updates and the two cannot be disentangled ([PMID 42382666](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42382666/)).

## A recipe for the C-arm level detector, and where it stands on adjacent evidence

The setting is a YOLO-family detector and a ResNet-scale per-vertebra classifier trained on physics-styled renders plus a few hundred real frames per device, where the transitional vertebra is the class that matters. The table gives one number per parameter, the evidence it rests on, and how far that evidence is from the setting.

Three of these numbers are firmer than the rest. The two-stage scheme with full-depth fine-tuning is supported by every comparison that exists, in and out of medicine, and contradicted by none. The 4:1 to 10:1 window is where four independent sweeps in three modalities flattened, and it agrees with the 5–20% real share that the one YOLO detector sweep found; the manuscript can cite it as the measured window while stating that no DRR study has run the sweep. And the distinct-cases lever is the only thing that has ever moved a transitional vertebra from undetectable to detectable in a public benchmark.

The rest stands on adjacent ground, and the manuscript should say so in as many words. No DRR or DeepDRR paper sweeps the synthetic-to-real ratio or the real-frame count; the sweeps are generative chest radiography, one guidewire study and street scenes. No fluoroscopy paper measures what 10, 50 or 100 real frames buy on a physics-rendered corpus; the 300–500 target is read off a DDPM radiograph curve and two fluoroscopy single points. No paper synthesises L6, T13 or Castellvi cases and reports per-class recall, so the 4× factor and the 25–35% prevalence are transplanted from CT segmentation and a CT challenge, and the factor has never been ablated even there. No paper ablates distinct CTs against renders per CT; the 200–500 renders figure is one curve at 20 fixed CTs that confounds the two. No X-ray paper measures upweighting, ramps or curricula against each other, so any schedule the project adopts is an experiment to report, not a recipe to cite. No study varies rendering fidelity and ratio together, and no one has tested physics-calibrated noise as augmentation of real frames for detection. And no YOLO learning curve on radiographs at 1024 px exists; the classifier knee near 20,000 images and the 256–448 px resolution optimum are chest-radiograph classification results.

Two measurement habits follow from the evidence rather than from the gaps. Report rare-class recall together with PR-AUC and common-class precision at a fixed operating point, because Dushenev's recall gain of 0.13 → 0.74 came with a PR-AUC loss of 0.846 → 0.677 and the ISIC 2019 baseline's rare-class sensitivities were "artificially inflated" at the majority class's expense ([Dushenev](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11898); [ISIC 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13337696/)). And fit the real-frame learning curve on the project's own device subsets — 50, 100, 200, 400 frames — rather than borrowing a crossover, since the scaling exponents that have been measured differ 3.5× across anatomies ([arXiv 2605.06859](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06859v1)); that curve, run once per device with the rare class held out separately, would be the first of its kind for DRR-trained fluoroscopy detectors.

## Conclusion

The literature's silence on the exact question is itself a finding the manuscript can use. The physics-rendering community standardised on synthetic-only training and real testing, and in doing so never asked how few real frames a rendered corpus needs; the generative-augmentation community swept ratios but on radiographs whose synthetic twins came from the same real distribution. Between them, the window that emerges — real frames at a tenth to a fifth of the mix, synthetic pretraining as large as the bank allows, real fine-tuning at full depth — is consistent across four modalities and two model families, and a spine-fluoroscopy detector trained that way would be operating inside every measured optimum without a single measurement from its own domain.

For the transitional vertebra the evidence reorders the priorities. Sampling weight is the cheap lever and 4× is the only value with a published result; but every time a rare vertebra became learnable it was because distinct cases were added, and the one class with six distinct scans never stabilised across two years of a public challenge. The render bank can raise the variant's prevalence to a third at no labelling cost, which is what VerSe'20 did with real scans, and that lever — choosing which CTs to render — has more support behind it than any oversampling factor. The experiment that would settle the rest, a real-frame learning curve per device with the transitional class scored separately and distinct CTs varied against renders per CT, has not been published for any DRR-trained X-ray detector, and this project is placed to run it.
