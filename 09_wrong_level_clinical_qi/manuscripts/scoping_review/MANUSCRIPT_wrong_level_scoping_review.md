## Introduction

Operating on the wrong vertebral level is the archetypal never-event of spine surgery, and it is unusual among
never-events in that its proximate cause is typically an imaging step rather than a marking or consent step.
Somebody counts vertebrae on an image, and the count is wrong.

Two features of spinal anatomy make that count fragile. The first is prevalence: a lumbosacral transitional
vertebra is present in a large minority of adults, with reported frequencies that track the modality used to
define them — about 29% in consecutive abdominal computed tomography, 26% in young-adult computed tomography,
about 9% on full-length spine radiographs, and about 3% on whole-spine magnetic resonance imaging. The second is
observability: a numerical variant, in which a patient has 23 or 25 presacral segments rather than 24, cannot be
recognised at all on imaging that does not span the whole spine, and standard lumbar protocols image neither the
thoracolumbar nor the lumbosacral junction in a way that permits enumeration.

The accuracy of the transitional call itself degrades sharply with modality. Against a reference standard set
with all three modalities, four blinded readers detected a transitional vertebra on computed tomography with 76%
sensitivity and inter-reader agreement of 0.63 to 0.71; on magnetic resonance imaging with 54% and 0.24 to 0.56;
and on conventional radiographs with 32% sensitivity, 42% accuracy and agreement of 0.16 to 0.32. Classification
into the Castellvi types is less reliable still on radiographs. In surgical series, level nomination made from
outpatient lumbar imaging was wrong in about half of patients with a transitional vertebra when checked against a
whole-spine radiograph.

Against this, the prevention literature reaches readily for organisational controls: the surgical time-out, site
marking, the universal protocol. Those controls address the steps that surround the image rather than the
determination of level within it. Whether the field neglects the imaging step, or writes about it without ever
testing what it recommends, has not been mapped, and the two have quite different remedies.

This review maps it. We ask what the literature reports about the frequency of wrong-level and wrong-site events,
the mechanisms authors name, the imaging step at which level determination fails, and what has been proposed or
evaluated to prevent it, with particular attention to whether transitional and numerical variants are implicated
and whether proposed countermeasures were ever evaluated against an outcome.

## Methods

Reported according to the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). The completed checklist is
Supplement 1.

### Protocol and registration

A protocol was prepared **after the searches were executed and after a preliminary descriptive count of the
corpus**, and before any record was charted, any human screening decision was recorded, or this manuscript was
drafted. It is Supplement 2. It was **not prospectively registered**, and no outcome reported here is
pre-specified; the synthesis is descriptive and exploratory throughout. Registration on the Open Science Framework
is intended before submission, carrying the same declaration.

### Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Human patients undergoing spine surgery or image-guided spinal intervention, or human imaging
studies whose subject is vertebral enumeration or transitional anatomy.

**Concept.** A wrong-level, wrong-site or incorrect-level event; its frequency, mechanism, or the imaging step by
which the operative level was determined; the accuracy or reliability of vertebral enumeration; or a method
intended to prevent a level error, including transitional-vertebra detection, numbering conventions,
intraoperative localisation and computer-assisted labelling.

**Context.** Any country, setting or year; any language with an English title and abstract.

Primary studies of any design were eligible, as were case reports, registry and closed-claims analyses, surveys
and technical evaluations carrying a clinical claim. Reviews were charted separately and are not counted as
primary evidence. We excluded animal-only or cadaver-only work without a clinical claim, wrong-site surgery
outside the spine, technical imaging papers without a level-determination claim, editorials and letters without
data, and records for which neither abstract nor full text could be retrieved.

### Information sources and search

Two sources were searched on 15 September 2026: Europe PMC, restricted to records carrying full text, and
Semantic Scholar. The Europe PMC query combined three blocks with AND — a variant-and-error block, an imaging
block and a spine block — and is reproduced verbatim in Supplement 3. Citation and reference expansion was
performed from transitional-anatomy seed papers. No hand-search of proceedings, no grey-literature search and no
author contact were undertaken.

### Selection of sources of evidence

Screening was **machine-assisted**, and we measured it rather than assuming it. In the first stage every record
was screened by explicit published patterns over the title and publication type, over the abstract (retrieved for
9,487 of 12,294 records; 2,807 have none in either source), and over the full text where it was held; each
decision
was recorded together with the exact strings that triggered it, so that no record was excluded by a process that
cannot be inspected (screening ledger, Supplement 4). In the second stage a random sample of 200 records,
stratified as 100 included and 100 excluded, was screened independently by two reviewers blinded to the automated
decision; from that sample we report the automated screen's sensitivity and specificity against the human
decision with exact binomial intervals, and inter-reviewer agreement as Cohen's kappa. In the third stage every
record reaching extraction was read by a human reviewer who confirmed eligibility and completed the charting form,
with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer.

### Data charting

A charting form was piloted on 20 records by two reviewers and revised before the full pass. Charted items were:
identifiers and setting; design; denominator and interval; numerator and the authors' event definition; reported
frequency verbatim; spinal region; whether transitional or numerical anatomy was implicated; mechanism, coded to
a named list; imaging modality, counting convention and any stated reference standard; and countermeasure, coded
to a named list together with its evaluation status — outcome-evaluated, confidence-evaluated, or proposed only.
Every charted field carries the sentence it came from and a flag recording whether the value was explicit,
inferred, or absent. An automated first pass pre-filled the form; the human entry is the record of truth, and we
report the proportion of automated fields a reviewer corrected.

### Synthesis of results

The primary synthesis is descriptive: counts and cross-tabulations of design, region, mechanism, imaging step and
countermeasure, with evaluation status shown alongside each countermeasure. A quantitative synthesis was planned
only for records reporting a numerator, a denominator and an event definition, using a random-effects model of
proportions on the logit scale with a Freeman-Tukey sensitivity analysis, heterogeneity as tau-squared and
I-squared, and prediction intervals. Subgroups planned in the protocol were region, design (registry or claims
versus institutional series) and era. **Whether a pooled estimate is reported at all was made conditional on
heterogeneity and definitional consistency**, and the decision with its reasoning is reported in the Results
either way. In keeping with PRISMA-ScR no risk-of-bias assessment was performed.

### What the charted data support, and what they do not

Every test below was run twice: on the subset where the field was stated explicitly, which is the primary
analysis, and on the wider set that admits inferred values, reported as a sensitivity analysis. Where the two
disagree, both are given and the disagreement is treated as a limit on what can be claimed. Full output is in
`REVIEW_STATS.md`.

**The asymmetry we expected is not demonstrated.** We tested whether sources naming an anatomical or radiological
mechanism were any more likely to name a radiological countermeasure than sources that did not. They were not, and
the test is underpowered: among the 45 sources that name a countermeasure explicitly, 72.2% (49.1 to 87.5) against
70.4% (51.5 to 84.1), Fisher p = 1.000, odds ratio 1.07 with an interval from 0.30 to 3.84. The sensitivity
analysis on 603 sources agrees and is equally null, odds ratio 0.95 (0.36 to 2.50). This is a failure to detect a
difference, not evidence that none exists; an interval spanning a threefold effect in either direction cannot
settle the question.

**What does separate, sharply, is whether a countermeasure was ever measured against an outcome.**

| countermeasure class | evaluated against an outcome |
|---|---|
| level determination: count from a landmark, marking, intraoperative radiograph, second read | **0 of 15 (0.0%, 0.0 to 20.4)** |
| navigation or computer assistance | 4 of 21 (19.0%, 7.7 to 40.0) |
| organisational: time-out, protocol, checklist | 7 of 18 (38.9%, 20.3 to 61.4) |

Fisher p = 0.024 for level determination against organisational, odds ratio 0.08 (0.00 to 1.45); the sensitivity
analysis on the full set gives 10.6% against 20.0%, p = 0.002. The countermeasures aimed directly at the step that
fails are named repeatedly and, in this corpus, never evaluated against an outcome. We had predicted instead that
the most-named countermeasures would be the least evaluated, and that prediction was wrong: the rank correlation
between how often a countermeasure is named and how often it is evaluated runs the other way (Spearman rho +0.78,
p = 0.069).

**The spread in reported frequency is real and this corpus cannot explain it.** Among the 43 rates stated adjacent
to the event they describe, the median is 500 per 10,000 and the span is 0.09 to 4,000, about 4.65 orders of
magnitude. Ascertainment is the obvious candidate, and the direction of the data matches it, but the evidence is
not there: rate against denominator size gives Spearman rho -0.10, p = 0.73 on 15 sources, and log rate by
denominator class gives Kruskal-Wallis H = 2.36, p = 0.125. The review can report the spread and name its likely
cause; it cannot quantify how much of it ascertainment explains.

**Transitional anatomy is implicated in about a fifth of sources reporting a wrong-level event**, 29 of 140, 20.7%
(14.8 to 28.2). A regional difference appears only in the sensitivity analysis, where lumbosacral sources implicate
it in 39.0% against 8.7% of thoracic ones, odds ratio 6.37 (3.20 to 12.69), p < 0.001; the explicit stratum is too
small to test.

**Attention to the imaging step is not clearly growing.** Measured as each year's proportion of included sources,
rather than as a count that would simply track the corpus, the explicit stratum is flat (Cochran-Armitage z = 0.40,
p = 0.691) while the sensitivity analysis rises (z = 5.76, p < 0.001). Organisational countermeasures are flat in
both. The two strata disagree here and on the regional question, which is reported rather than resolved.

### Reported frequency, and why it is not pooled

**A proportion synthesis is not available from this corpus, and that is itself a result.** Of 1,933 included
sources, four carry both a numerator and a denominator, and exactly one states them in a single sentence (25
wrong-site procedures among 2,826,367 operations). A denominator drawn from one sentence and a numerator from
another are two numbers in one paper, not a proportion, and were not treated as one.

What the literature reports instead is a rate in prose. 426 sources state a frequency verbatim, and 75 state one
in a sentence that also names a wrong-level or wrong-site event. Their denominators are not one kind of thing:
procedures in 25, surgeons surveyed in 16, unstated in 15, patients in 9, wrong-level cases in 5, reported events
in 3 and malpractice claims in 2. Only procedure- and patient-denominated rates are commensurable at all, and
those number 34; each keeps its unit, and a frequency written as a range is kept as a range rather than reduced
to a midpoint.

**Among the rates that are both normalisable and stated next to the event they describe, reported frequency spans
four orders of magnitude, from 0.09 to 4,000 per 10,000 procedures.** The two ends are not in conflict; they
measure different things. The low end comes from registry and malpractice-claims series, where an event enters
the record only if it was recognised, reported, and survived to a claim: one in 112,000 procedures, one in 3,110
spinal fusions. The high end comes from small surgical series that inspected every operated level, where a wrong
initial exposure was found in a sixth of cases and corrected before the definitive procedure. The literature is
not disagreeing about how often surgeons operate at the wrong level. It is reporting two different quantities:
events that became visible to an institution, and errors that occurred and were caught. A pooled figure would
average them silently.

We therefore report the range with its composition rather than a pooled estimate, and the pre-specified
random-effects synthesis was not performed. Pooling rates whose denominators are not the same kind of thing, for
an event whose ascertainment is incomplete by construction, would produce a number more precise than the
evidence.

### The imaging resources that exist to build and test such a control

The discussion above ends in a specification: a control at the imaging step must raise the suspicion that the
anatomy is atypical and demand a count the single image cannot supply, and it must be evaluated against an
outcome. Whether that study can be run at all depends on what labelled imaging exists. We therefore charted the
public radiographic collections that carry per-vertebra annotation, using the same charting discipline as the
source review: what is stated in the release, what we verified ourselves, and what rests on an assumption.

Thirteen collections were assembled and examined, totalling approximately 66,000 spinal radiographs. Table 7 gives
them. Four facts in it bear on whether the specification can be met.

**Table 7.** Radiographic collections carrying, or capable of carrying, per-vertebra level labels.

| collection | films | annotation | levels recoverable | level naming verified |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BUU-LSPINE | 3,600 patients, 7,204 images | endplate lines | L1–L5, S1 | by release convention |
| CSXA | 4,963 | per-vertebra | cervical only | by release convention |
| NHANES II, annotated subset | 543 | landmarks | C2–T1 and T11–S1 | by release convention |
| NHANES II, remainder | 16,530 | none | none without pseudo-labelling | not applicable |
| VinDr-SpineXR | 744 | boxes | C3–C7, L1–L5 | by release convention |
| AASCE / BoostNet | 481 labelled of 579 | 68 landmarks, 17 bodies | T1–L5 | published convention; independent model check inconclusive |
| Mendeley 5jdfdgp762 | 584 usable | 4 corners per body | T8–sacrum | verified against an independent annotation of the same films |
| IEEE DataPort lateral full-spine | 236 | none | none without pseudo-labelling | not applicable |
| Spinal-AI2024 | 20,001, machine-generated | machine-generated corners | T1–L5 if assumed | **not verifiable** |
| BUU Drive mirror | 0 new | — | — | byte-identical duplicate |

**Real radiographs labelled at mid-thoracic levels are almost absent from public data.** Of the collections above,
exactly one images and labels the mid-thoracic spine on real film: AASCE, at 481 films. NHANES II, by far the
largest real resource at 17,073 films, contains no vertebra between T2 and T10 in either of its two acquisitions.
BUU, CSXA, VinDr and the Mendeley set are cervical or lumbosacral. The only other collection covering the whole
thoracic spine on real film carries no annotation at all. This matters directly for the review's subject: the
thoracic spine is where counting is hardest and where the intraoperative radiograph is least informative, and it
is the region for which the least labelled real data exists to build or test anything.

**Naming a vertebra by counting requires knowing which vertebra the count starts at, and that anchor is where a
constant error hides.** Every collection above whose labels are corner or landmark coordinates is class-agnostic:
it marks bodies, not names. A name is produced by counting from an assumed anchor, and an anchor chosen wrongly
shifts every label by the same amount. Such an error is perfectly self-consistent and survives any internal check.
We report two attempts to test an anchor from the geometry alone, because both failed and the failures are
informative.

*Spacing-profile matching cannot identify a start vertebra.* Matching the inter-body spacing profile of one
collection to a reference collection with known names gave a discrepancy of 0.0098, which reads as agreement until
the reference collection is matched against itself shifted by exactly one vertebra: that gives 0.0027, a better
score than the true match. The spacing of a spinal column is close to scale-free over the thoracolumbar region, so
this statistic has no power to identify an anchor, in this dataset or any other.

*An interior-gap gate has no power on anterior-posterior films of curved spines.* The ratio of maximum to median
inter-body spacing, used to detect a missed vertebra, is 1.41 with a 90th centile of 1.81 on a collection's own
human landmarks, against 1.58 and 1.81 on the collection under test. No threshold separates a missed vertebra from
the curve itself. The same statistic is 1.06 on lateral lumbar films, where the gate does work. A gate validated
on one view and modality does not transfer to another.

Where an anchor could be checked against an independent annotation of the same films, it was. For the Mendeley
collection, a second group's annotation of 501 shared films adjudicated between the two candidate anchors on the
317 films where they diverge: the caudal anchor won on 295 and the cranial on 22. For AASCE, the published
convention was tested against a level classifier trained on a corpus containing none of those films, and the test
returned no verdict: that classifier collapses to the lumbar classes on anterior-posterior films and could not
separate the candidate offsets, scoring 10.6% against 9.6% for the runner-up. The naming rests on the published
convention and on visual inspection of rendered overlays, and we say so rather than describing it as validated.

**A large synthetic collection has far less effective diversity than its size suggests, and its published split
does not separate.** Spinal-AI2024 releases 20,001 machine-generated spinal radiographs with machine-generated
annotations. Its own shipped similarity checklist maps every film to a nearest real film, and the 20,440 entries
collapse onto 52 distinct real films, one of which is nearest to 2,988 generated images. Further, 4,438 of the
4,440 films in the released test subset share a nearest real film with a training film, so the released split does
not separate training from test at the level of the underlying anatomy. We report this not to criticise the
resource, which is explicit about being generated, but because a level classifier trained and validated on it
would produce a validation number with no held-out anatomy behind it.

### Strongly labelled radiographs against weakly labelled fluoroscopy

The review's specification requires a reference standard fixed before the comparison. The imaging available to
this project falls into two classes that are not interchangeable, and treating them as one would produce exactly
the falsely precise number the frequency synthesis was withheld to avoid.

The first class is radiographs whose labels come from a human annotation file or from a labelled cross-sectional
volume. The second is fluoroscopic and radiographic panels mined from the published literature, whose labels are
decoder placements constrained by outside evidence in the article: the caption, optical character recognition of
burned-in text, the hardware visible in the frame, and the levels named in neighbouring panels of the same figure.
An agreement figure on the second class is agreement with a weak label, not accuracy against a reading, and it is
reported as such throughout.

`[[N13]]` gives the per-level comparison between the two classes for a single classifier evaluated on both through
the same code path. Two features of it are stable across the arms tested and are worth stating in advance of the
table: agreement on the weakly labelled fluoroscopy panels is far below accuracy on the strongly labelled films,
and the gap is not uniform across the spine. The weak labels are themselves concentrated at the levels that
captions name, which are the levels at which surgery is reported, so the comparison is between two different
distributions as well as two different label qualities.

## Discussion

> Written against the charted results; the argument below is the frame, and any claim that the numbers do not
> support will be removed rather than softened.

Three questions organise the discussion, and the first one's answer is not the one we expected.

**Does the prevention literature address the step that fails?** We cannot show that it does not. The test of the
asymmetry is null and underpowered, and a preliminary count of passages that suggested otherwise did not survive
charting: counting mentions in text is not the same as testing whether sources that name an imaging mechanism also
name an imaging remedy, and when the question is put properly the answer is that this corpus cannot tell. That is
worth stating plainly, because the intuition is widely shared and is not, on this evidence, established.

**What is established is narrower and, we think, more useful: the countermeasures aimed at the failing step have
never been measured against an outcome.** Counting from a fixed landmark, marking, the intraoperative radiograph
and the second read are named across the literature and evaluated against an outcome in none of the 15 sources
where the class was explicit, against 38.9% for organisational controls and 19.0% for navigation (p = 0.024). The
field has not neglected the imaging step in its writing; it has neglected to test what it recommends there. A
reader of this literature can learn that counting from C2 is advised, and cannot learn whether it reduces the rate
of wrong-level surgery, because nobody has reported that comparison.

**What would a control at the failing step have to do?** The reference-standard literature constrains it sharply.
On a single lateral radiograph the only measurement with published radiographic performance detects a transitional
segment with a solid bony bridge and cannot see the broad transverse process that accounts for most transitional
anatomy; the Castellvi type is not reliably assignable from a radiograph; and a numerical variant cannot be
established from a field of view that excludes the thoracolumbar or lumbosacral junction. A control at this step
can honestly do one thing: raise the suspicion that the anatomy is atypical and demand a count the single image
cannot supply. Anything stronger overstates what the image contains. The absence of outcome evaluation in this
class is therefore not only a gap in the literature but a specification for the study that would close it.

**Can the study that closes the gap actually be run?** Partly, and the limit is data rather than method. A control
that raises suspicion of atypical anatomy and demands a count can be built and evaluated at the lumbosacral
junction, where several thousand annotated real radiographs exist and where transitional anatomy is implicated
several times more often. It cannot presently be evaluated in the mid-thoracic spine on real film, because one
public collection of 481 radiographs is all that carries labels there. That is a more specific statement of the
gap than "more data is needed": the region where counting is hardest is the region with almost no labelled real
radiographs, and an evaluation restricted to the lumbosacral junction should be reported as such rather than
described as a spine-wide result.

**A methodological caution that generalises beyond this review.** Two geometric tests of a level-naming anchor
were attempted and both were shown to have no power, one of them only after it had returned a confident-looking
number. A spine's inter-body spacing is close to scale-free, so profile matching cannot identify which vertebra a
count begins at; and a gap statistic that detects a missed vertebra on lateral lumbar films does not do so on
anterior-posterior films of curved spines. Any claim that a dataset's level names have been checked should say
what the check was and what it would have detected, because a check with no power returns agreement.

**How often does this happen?** The honest answer from this corpus is that the reported frequency spans more than
four orders of magnitude and that the literature is reporting two different quantities under one name: events that
became visible to an institution, and errors that occurred and were caught. We could not demonstrate how much of
the spread ascertainment explains, and we report the range with its composition rather than a pooled figure that
would conceal the problem.

**Transitional anatomy** is implicated in about a fifth of sources reporting an event, and in the wider stratum
that proportion is several times higher at the lumbosacral junction than in the thoracic spine. That is consistent
with the anatomy: the junction is where the variants are, and where a count that assumes 24 presacral segments
fails.

## Limitations

The search was built for an engineering project and repurposed, so it was not designed hypothesis-blind, and the
protocol is post hoc. The Europe PMC arm was restricted to records with full text, which under-covers
closed-access journals where several of the field's most-cited series sit; Semantic Scholar partly compensates but
supplies abstracts rather than full text for many records. No grey literature, proceedings or author contact.
Screening was machine-assisted, and although its performance is measured on a stratified sample, a pattern-based
screen cannot recognise a relevant record that uses none of its terms. No risk-of-bias assessment was performed,
in keeping with PRISMA-ScR, so this review maps what has been claimed rather than what is established. Event
ascertainment in the underlying literature is incomplete by construction. The charting of imaging resources in Table 7 covers collections we were able to obtain and examine directly; it is not a systematic search of dataset repositories, and a collection we did not find is not evidence that none exists.
Finally, the authors are building a
method in this domain, which is a competing interest of intellectual commitment as much as of finance; it is
declared, and it is the reason the screening decisions, the charting table and the code are released in full.

## What this review cannot do

It cannot show that any method, including one developed by the authors, is superior to another. It maps claims,
compares no interventions and applies no risk-of-bias assessment. A superiority claim requires a benchmark
against named comparators, on data the comparators' authors did not select, with a reference standard fixed
before the comparison. This review's contribution toward that study is to define the gap it must fill and the
performance envelope it must beat.
